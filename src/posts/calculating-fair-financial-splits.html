To calculate a fair financial split, you need three agreed inputs: each partner's income (usually take-home pay rather than gross), the list of costs that count as shared, and rules for the awkward items such as children, pensions, bonuses and debts. Once those are settled, the arithmetic is one line: each person's share of the income times the shared costs. Almost every disagreement about fairness is really a disagreement about the inputs.

This article is about those inputs. For the formula worked step by step, see income-proportional bill splitting, and for a comparison of the four main methods, see how to split bills when you earn different amounts.

Should you use gross or net income to split bills?

Use net take-home pay in most cases. It is the money each of you actually has to spend. Gross income ignores the fact that higher earners usually lose a larger share to tax, so splitting on gross asks the higher earner to pay a little more than their real share.

Sam and Alex earn £60,000 and £36,000 a year before tax. Suppose that after tax and other compulsory deductions they take home £3,600 and £2,400 a month. (These are illustrative figures; use the numbers on your own payslips.) Their shared costs are £2,500 a month.

BasisSam's shareSam paysAlex pays
Gross (£60,000 ÷ £96,000)62.5%£1,562.50£937.50
Net (£3,600 ÷ £6,000)60%£1,500.00£1,000.00

The difference is £62.50 a month. Not dramatic, but it compounds into £750 a year, and the gap widens as the income difference grows. Gross is simpler if you are paid in different ways that make take-home hard to compare. Net is closer to reality. Whichever you use, use the same basis for both people.

Which costs count as shared?

Anything the household uses together is shared. Anything that serves one person is personal. Most items sort themselves; a handful need a rule.

Clearly shared

  • Rent or mortgage, council tax, buildings and contents insurance
  • Energy, water, broadband, the TV licence
  • Groceries and household supplies
  • Repairs, maintenance and furniture for shared spaces
  • Subscriptions you both use

Clearly personal

  • Your own clothes, haircuts, gym membership and hobbies
  • Your mobile phone contract
  • Lunches and coffees at work
  • Gifts you give personally

The grey areas, and common ways to settle them

ItemA common rule
CarShared if you both rely on it; personal if it is one person's commute vehicle. Some couples split it by rough share of use.
Commuting costsUsually personal, since they are a cost of earning that person's income.
Meals out and holidays togetherShared in the same ratio, or treated as a separate joint budget you agree in advance.
PetsShared if the pet is jointly yours; personal if it came with one partner and the other never wanted it.
Gifts to each side of the familyShared if you give as a couple; personal if each of you gives to your own family.

The rule matters less than the fact that you wrote it down. The same grey item argued about every month is a sign the rule was never agreed.

How do you handle children's costs in a fair split?

For your shared children, treat their costs as household costs and split them by the same method as everything else. Nursery fees, clothes, school trips and activities all go in the shared pot.

Three situations need more thought:

  • Childcare that enables one partner to work. Some couples argue it should be charged to the partner whose working hours it covers. That logic tends to penalise whoever earns less, usually the parent who already reduced their hours. The simplest approach is to treat childcare as a cost of having children, shared like any other.
  • Children from a previous relationship. Whether a step-parent contributes to a stepchild's costs is a values question with no single right answer. Child maintenance received or paid also needs to be counted somewhere, usually against the parent it relates to. Blended families and step-parenting covers this in detail.
  • Child-related benefits or allowances. Decide whether they count as one parent's income or go straight into the shared pot. Straight into the pot is simplest.

What about pensions, bonuses and other income?

Count the money each of you can actually spend each month, and agree a rule for anything irregular or optional.

Pension contributions

Compulsory or standard workplace contributions come out before take-home pay, and most couples leave that as it is. Voluntary extra contributions are different. If Alex pays an extra £300 a month into a pension, Alex's take-home drops from £2,400 to £2,100, and under a proportional split Alex's share of the bills drops too, so Sam is effectively helping fund Alex's retirement saving. Some couples are happy with that as a joint goal. Others add voluntary contributions back before calculating. Agree which, and do the same for both people. For pension decisions themselves, a qualified financial adviser is the right person to ask.

Bonuses and overtime

Either average them into monthly income across the year, or leave them out of the bills split and agree separately how they are used: into joint savings in the same ratio, for example.

Other income

Rental income, investment income and side work usually count if they are regular. A one-off windfall is usually personal unless you agree otherwise.

Existing debts

Some couples deduct required repayments on debts that predate the relationship from income before splitting. It changes the ratio, so agree it openly. If debt is a significant worry, a free debt advice charity is a better guide than a formula, and managing debt as a couple covers the conversation.

A worked calculation with every input

Here is Sam and Alex's full calculation, with each decision noted.

  1. Income basis: net take-home, standard pension contributions already deducted, no voluntary extras. Sam £3,600, Alex £2,400. Combined £6,000.
  2. Ratio: Sam £3,600 ÷ £6,000 = 60%. Alex 40%.
  3. Shared household costs: rent £1,400, council tax and utilities £380, food £520, insurance and broadband £100, shared car £100. Total £2,500.
  4. Children's costs: nursery and other costs for their son, £500. Treated as shared.
  5. Total to split: £2,500 + £500 = £3,000.
  6. Each pays: Sam £3,000 × 0.60 = £1,800. Alex £3,000 × 0.40 = £1,200.
  7. Left over: Sam £1,800, Alex £1,200. Both have put half their take-home pay into the household.

If £1,200 is tight for Alex, the next step is not to fiddle with the inputs but to consider a protected personal allowance first, as the equal remainder method does. Common questions

Do you include savings in the shared costs?

Joint savings goals, such as a house deposit or an emergency fund, can be treated as a shared cost and split in the same ratio. Personal savings stay personal. Keep them on separate lines so you can see each clearly.

How do you split costs if one partner works from home?

Some couples ask the home-worker to cover the extra energy their working day uses, especially if an employer contributes towards it. Others treat it as shared because the home-worker is not paying for a commute. Agree an approach and apply it consistently.

What if our incomes change every month?

Use an average of the last twelve months to set the split, then true it up each quarter against actual income. The method is worked through in detail in the proportional splitting guide.
