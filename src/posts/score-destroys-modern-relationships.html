Keeping score in a relationship backfires because the score is always wrong in your favour. You see all of your own effort and only part of your partner's, so both of you can sincerely believe you are ahead. Tallies also treat a five-minute chore and a week of planning as equal, and they turn two people sharing a home into opponents. Scorekeeping is usually a sign that something real feels unfair. The tally just makes it harder to fix.

The useful alternative is not to stop paying attention. It is to swap a private tally for a shared picture.

What does keeping score in a relationship look like?

It is the running count in your head of who did what. Sometimes it is said out loud: "I did bedtime three nights running." More often it is silent, and surfaces only in arguments, where it arrives fully formed.

Common forms include counting chores, counting money ("I paid for the last two dinners"), counting favours ("I went to your work party, so you owe me my sister's birthday") and counting rest ("you had a lie-in on Saturday and Sunday"). Each one feels like fairness. Each one is a ledger kept by one side.

Why does keeping score backfire?

Four reasons, and they compound.

1. Your memory is biased towards your own work

You were present for every minute of your own effort and absent for much of your partner's. You remember waiting in for the plumber. You do not remember your partner's forty minutes on the phone to the bank. This is an ordinary feature of memory, not dishonesty, and it means two fair people each overestimate their own share.

2. Tasks are not equal units

A tally counts items. Taking the bins out is one item. Organising a child's birthday party is also one item, but it is hours of doing and weeks of background planning. Any count treats them the same, which is why counting tends to undervalue whoever does the planning. What the mental load is explains why that work is so easy to miss.

3. A score needs a loser

Once there is a score, every chore is a point won or lost. Doing something kind becomes a move in a game. The language shifts to debt: "you owe me", "I'm owed a night off". Debt between partners breeds resentment on both sides of the ledger.

4. It hides the real question

The real question is rarely "who did more this week?" It is "do we both have enough of our own life left?" A tally cannot answer that, because it ignores paid work, commuting, health and capacity. A partner who works sixty hours and does fewer chores may still have less free time than the one who does more at home.

Then add paid work. Sam works 30 hours a week from home. Alex works 40 hours plus 5 hours of commuting. Sam's paid work and housework come to 40 hours; Alex's come to 51. On these numbers, Alex has about 11 fewer hours of their own each week, even though Sam does more chores. Neither tally captured that. Both were honest; both were wrong.

Is all scorekeeping bad?

No. Noticing imbalance is healthy. Keeping a private tally of it is what backfires. If one person is carrying far more, saying so is important, and "let's not keep score" can become a way of dismissing a real problem.

How do you stop keeping score?

Replace the tally with three habits.

  1. Name the arrangement, not the person. "Bedtime has been with me every night this month" is a fact about the system. "You never do bedtime" is a verdict, and verdicts start defences.
  2. Look at the whole week together, once. Paid work, commuting, housework and planning, written down by both of you. Most scorekeeping fades when both people can see the same thing.
  3. Change one thing, then wait. Move one area of work, live with it for a few weeks, and only then look again. Constant recalculation is just scorekeeping with better maths.

If the scorekeeping has been going on for a long time, there is often resentment underneath it that a new system will not touch on its own. Common questions

Is keeping score a sign of a bad relationship?

Not necessarily. It is usually a sign that one or both of you feel something is unfair and do not have a better way of showing it. Treat it as information about the arrangement.

What is the difference between keeping score and fairness?

Keeping score compares tasks or favours one by one. Fairness asks whether both of you end up with a similar amount of time and energy of your own, over weeks rather than days.

Should couples keep score with money?

Tracking shared spending is sensible; counting who paid for which dinner is not. A clear method for shared bills, such as splitting in proportion to income, removes the need for a running tally. How to split bills when you earn different amounts sets out the options.

What do I do if my partner keeps score?

Suggest building one shared picture of the week together instead of trading counts. Ask what feels unfair to them, because the tally is usually pointing at something real.
