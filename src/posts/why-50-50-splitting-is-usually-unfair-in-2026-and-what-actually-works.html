Splitting bills 50/50 is usually unfair because paying the same amount isn't the same cost to each of you. When incomes differ, the lower earner hands over a much bigger share of their pay and is left with far less of their own, and the one with more spare money tends to end up making the calls. Splitting in proportion to income usually fixes this. If your incomes are close, 50/50 is fine.

The trouble with 50/50 is that it looks neutral. Most couples never sit down and pick it; it's just where they started, and nobody questions it until one of them has been quietly short for a couple of years.

Why an equal split feels fair but works out unfair

Because it measures what each person pays, while what each person actually lives on is what they have left. Two people can pay exactly the same and have very different months.

Take Maya and Jordan. Maya takes home £4,000 a month and Jordan £2,000. Their shared costs, meaning rent, bills and groceries, come to £2,400.

  • On 50/50, each pays £1,200.
  • For Maya, £1,200 is 30% of her take-home pay. For Jordan it's 60%.
  • Maya has £2,800 left. Jordan has £800.

Over a year, that's £33,600 left for Maya and £9,600 for Jordan, a gap of £24,000. They both "pay half", yet Maya has three and a half times as much to live on.

What 50/50 does over a few years

The income gap slowly becomes a savings gap, and then a gap in who gets a say. The monthly sums are only the start.

The savings gap compounds

Maya can save each month. Jordan has £800 for everything personal, including travel, clothes and seeing friends, so he probably can't. Over five years that becomes a large difference in who owns what, and if the relationship ever ends, that difference is very real.

Decisions drift to one person

When only one of you can afford to say yes, that person decides. Holidays, a nicer sofa, whether the broken washing machine gets repaired or replaced. Nobody calls these power decisions, but over time they add up to one person steering the household. The lower earner can start to feel like a lodger in their own home. Nobody says it out loud

The partner who's short rarely raises it, because objecting to an equal split can feel like asking for a favour. So it comes out sideways, in a tense evening over a takeaway or a weekend away. How to talk about money without fighting helps when it finally does come up.

What works better: splitting in proportion to income

For most couples with a real income gap, split shared costs in proportion to income. You each pay the same share of your pay, so the amounts differ but the weight is the same.

Back to Maya and Jordan. Together they take home £6,000. Maya earns two thirds of it and Jordan one third.

Maya still keeps twice as much as Jordan, because she earns twice as much. Nobody is subsidising anybody. Jordan just isn't spending most of his pay on a home they share equally any more.

If proportional doesn't feel quite right either, there are two more options: protecting a personal allowance for each partner first, or splitting so you both have the same amount left. When a 50/50 split is fair

When your take-home pay is close, your shared costs are modest and you both have a comfortable amount left. Then the simplicity is worth more than the small difference.

A useful rule of thumb: if your incomes are within about 15% of each other, 50/50 is fine. Beyond that, work out what each of you has left under both methods and look at the numbers together. If one of you would be tight on 50/50, the split is doing something neither of you meant it to.

50/50 also works for couples who choose it with open eyes. If the lower earner prefers paying half, and can afford it, that's a perfectly good choice. What causes trouble is 50/50 that nobody ever actually chose.

The costs that sit outside the split

Even a careful split can be undone by spending nobody writes down. Two patterns come up a lot.

  • Small shared spending. One partner always buys the milk, the birthday presents for the in-laws and the replacement lightbulbs. None of it goes on the shared list, and over a year it adds up, so count it.
  • Career trade-offs. One partner went part-time after a baby or turned down a move. The household benefits, and that partner's income, savings and pension take the hit. A 50/50 split charges them as if it never happened.

Unpaid work matters here too, but don't try to price it and offset it against bills. Agreeing an hourly rate for your partner's time turns every chore into a transaction. Measure housework and money separately and look at both. How to switch from 50/50 without a row

Start with the numbers. A table of what each of you has left is much easier to talk about than a vague sense that something's off.

  1. Choose a quiet day when no bill is due.
  2. Each write down your take-home pay and the shared costs.
  3. Work out what each of you has left under 50/50 and under proportional.
  4. Ask which result you would both be comfortable defending to a friend.
  5. Agree a date to review it, and a list of changes that trigger an early review.

How you put it makes a difference. "The split we fell into leaves you with much more than me" is about the arrangement. "You're not paying enough" sounds personal, and it usually gets a defensive answer. How to split bills as a couple has the practical set-up once you have agreed.

Common questions

Is it fair for the higher earner to pay all the bills?

It can be, if you both agree and the lower earner covers other things or you have merged finances. Without that agreement it tends to create the same imbalance in reverse, with one person feeling like the household's bank.

Should rent be split 50/50 if one room is bigger?

Room size matters more for housemates than for couples sharing a bedroom. For couples, the income gap usually matters more than the floor plan. 50/50 versus proportional rent splitting compares the options.

Do we use gross or net income for a proportional split?

Use take-home pay, after tax and pension contributions. That's the money you actually have to spend.
