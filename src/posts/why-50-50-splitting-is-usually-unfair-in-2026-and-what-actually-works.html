Splitting bills 50/50 is usually unfair because equal payments are not equal costs. When incomes differ, the lower earner hands over a much bigger share of their pay and is left with far less of their own, so one partner ends up funding the household's choices while the other ends up deciding them. Splitting in proportion to income usually fixes this. When your incomes are close, 50/50 is fine.

The trouble with 50/50 is that it looks neutral. Nobody chooses it after weighing the alternatives. It is the default, and defaults do not get questioned until someone has been quietly short for a couple of years.

Why does an equal split feel fair but work out unfair?

Because it measures what each person pays, when what each person feels is what they have left. Two people can pay exactly the same and live very different months.

Take Maya and Jordan. Maya takes home £4,000 a month and Jordan £2,000. Their shared costs, meaning rent, bills and groceries, come to £2,400.

  • On 50/50, each pays £1,200.
  • For Maya, £1,200 is 30% of her take-home pay. For Jordan it is 60%.
  • Maya has £2,800 left. Jordan has £800.

Over a year, that is £33,600 left for Maya and £9,600 for Jordan, a gap of £24,000. Both of them "pay half", and one of them has three and a half times as much money to live on.

What does 50/50 do over time?

It turns an income gap into a savings gap, and a savings gap into a power gap. The arithmetic is only the start.

The savings gap compounds

Maya can save each month. Jordan, with £800 for everything personal including travel, clothes and seeing friends, probably cannot. Over five years that becomes a large difference in who owns what, and if the relationship ends, that difference is real.

Decisions drift to one person

When only one of you can afford to say yes, that person decides. Holidays, a nicer sofa, whether the broken washing machine gets repaired or replaced: none of these are discussed as power, but that is what they become. The lower earner starts to feel like a lodger in their own home.

It stays unspoken

The partner who is short rarely raises it, because complaining about an equal split sounds like asking for a favour. So it surfaces sideways, in arguments about a takeaway or a weekend away. How to talk about money without fighting helps when it finally does come up.

What works better than splitting 50/50?

For most couples with a real income gap, split shared costs in proportion to income. Each person pays the same share of their pay, so the burden is equal even though the amounts are not.

Back to Maya and Jordan. Together they take home £6,000. Maya earns two thirds of it and Jordan one third.

Maya still keeps twice as much as Jordan, because she earns twice as much. Nobody is subsidising anybody. The difference is that Jordan is no longer spending most of his pay on a home they share equally.

There are two further options when proportional is not enough: protecting a personal allowance for each partner first, or splitting so you both have the same amount left. When is a 50/50 split actually fair?

When your take-home pay is close, your shared costs are modest, and both of you have a comfortable amount left. In that case the simplicity is worth more than the small difference.

A useful rule of thumb: if your incomes are within about 15% of each other, 50/50 is fine. Beyond that, work out what each of you has left under both methods and look at the numbers together. If one of you would be tight on 50/50, the method is doing something neither of you intended.

50/50 also works for couples who choose it with open eyes. If the lower earner prefers paying half, and it is affordable, that is a legitimate choice. The problem is not 50/50. It is 50/50 that was never chosen.

What about the hidden costs nobody counts?

Even a careful split can be undone by what sits outside it. Two patterns show up again and again.

  • Small shared spending. One partner always buys the milk, the birthday presents for the in-laws and the replacement lightbulbs. None of it goes on the shared list, and it adds up. Count it.
  • Career trade-offs. One partner went part-time after a baby or turned down a move. The household benefits, and that partner's income, savings and pension take the hit. A 50/50 split charges them as if it never happened.

Unpaid work matters here too, but do not try to price it and offset it against bills. Agreeing an hourly rate for your partner's time turns every chore into a transaction. Measure housework and money separately and look at both. How do you switch from 50/50 without a row?

Bring the numbers, not the grievance. A table of what each of you has left is easier to discuss than a feeling that something is off.

  1. Choose a quiet day when no bill is due.
  2. Each write down your take-home pay and the shared costs.
  3. Work out what each of you has left under 50/50 and under proportional.
  4. Ask which result you would both be comfortable defending to a friend.
  5. Agree a date to review it, and a list of changes that trigger an early review.

Framing matters. "The split we fell into leaves you with much more than me" is about the arrangement. "You are not paying enough" is about the person, and it gets a defensive answer. How to split bills as a couple has the practical set-up once you have agreed.

Common questions

Is it fair for the higher earner to pay all the bills?

It can be, if you both agree and the lower earner covers other things or you have merged finances. Without agreement it tends to create the same imbalance in reverse, with one person feeling like the household's funder.

Should rent be split 50/50 if one room is bigger?

Room size matters more for housemates than for couples sharing a bedroom. For couples, the income gap usually matters more than the floor plan. 50/50 versus proportional rent splitting compares the options.

Do we use gross or net income for a proportional split?

Use take-home pay, after tax and pension contributions. That is the money actually available to spend.
