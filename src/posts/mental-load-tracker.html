A mental load tracker is any method for recording who in a household does the planning, remembering and monitoring, not only who does the physical chores. The useful ones work on estimates and ownership rather than stopwatches: they list the planning tasks, note who holds each one, give it a rough weekly time, and compare how both partners see it. Precision matters less than both of you looking at the same picture.

You can do this on paper, in a spreadsheet or in an app. The method matters more than the tool, so this guide covers four approaches you can start this week and what each one can and cannot see.

What does a mental load tracker actually measure?

It measures thinking work that produces no visible result. Allison Daminger's 2019 research on the cognitive side of household labour split it into four parts: anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding, and monitoring. A good tracker tries to capture all four.

In practice that means tasks like these:

  • Planning the week's meals and writing the shopping list
  • Keeping track of school dates, forms and payments
  • Noticing what is running out before it runs out
  • Remembering birthdays and buying presents
  • Booking appointments for children, pets and the car
  • Watching renewals for insurance, broadband and subscriptions

A normal chore chart lists "cook dinner". A mental load tracker also lists "decide what we are eating this week", because that is a separate job and often a different person. Cognitive labour versus physical chores explains why both halves need recording.

Can you really put a number on mental load?

Roughly, yes. Precisely, no, and any tracker that claims precision is overselling. Nobody can log the twenty seconds it took to notice the milk was low.

What you can do is estimate. Meal planning for a family might take about half an hour of focused time a week, plus a background hum of attention. Tracking school admin might be twenty minutes a week in a busy term. Those estimates will be wrong in the details and right in the overall shape, which is what you need. The point is not an exact figure. It is to stop the conversation from being "I do everything" versus "no you don't".

Four ways to track the mental load

Pick one and run it for two weeks. Each has a different strength.

1. The open-loop audit (15 minutes, once)

Sit down separately and each write every household thing you are currently keeping track of: renewals, appointments, things that need replacing, people who need a call. Then compare lists. The point is not who wrote more. It is which items appear on only one list, because those are the loops the other person did not know existed.

2. The ownership map

List the main planning areas of your home and, for each, write who holds it: me, mostly me, shared, mostly my partner, or my partner. Do it separately, then compare. When you both write "mostly me" for the same area, you have found a perception gap. That is not proof that one of you is wrong. It usually means each of you sees your own part and not the other's.

3. The two-week estimate log

Once a week, list the planning tasks that happened and write a rough time against each. Do not time them as you go. Fill it in from memory in one sitting, which is more honest and far more likely to actually get done.

4. A weighted chore list

Add planning tasks to your normal chore list, but mark them as planning-heavy so they count for more than a quick physical job of the same length. This is the closest thing to a full picture, because it puts the doing and the thinking on one page.

A worked example: Maya and Jordan

Here is what an ownership map and estimate log looked like for one household after two weeks.

Maya and Jordan both work full-time and have a seven-year-old. Jordan does most of the cooking and all of the laundry. Maya assumed the planning was mostly hers. Jordan assumed it was shared.

Their estimates for the planning side came out like this:

  • Meal planning and shopping list: 45 minutes a week. Jordan.
  • School admin, forms and dates: 40 minutes. Maya.
  • Social calendar and presents: 30 minutes. Maya.
  • Bills, renewals and paperwork: 30 minutes. Maya.
  • Booking appointments: 20 minutes. Maya.

That is 45 minutes for Jordan and 2 hours for Maya (40 + 30 + 30 + 20 = 120 minutes). On physical chores Jordan was ahead by about three hours a week. So neither of them was simply right. Jordan was doing more of the doing, Maya more of the planning, and the planning had never been counted. Seeing both on one page changed the conversation from "who does more" to "should school admin move to Jordan, and laundry partly to Maya?"

What a mental load tracker cannot measure

Be honest about the limits, because overclaiming is how a tracker becomes a weapon.

  • Background attention. The constant low hum of holding a list is real and no log captures it fully.
  • Emotional labour. Managing moods and smoothing tension is important work, but scoring it tends to turn feelings into a ledger. Most careful trackers leave it out on purpose.
  • Whether the time felt chosen. An hour planning a holiday you love is different from an hour chasing a late form.
  • Broken time. Four separate interruptions are more tiring than one block of the same length.

Common questions

Is there a free mental load tracker?

Yes. A shared note or spreadsheet with the ownership map and a weekly estimate log works well. The free household fairness calculator also gives a quick read of free hours with no account.

How long should we track before talking about it?

Two weeks is usually enough to see the shape. One week can be skewed by a deadline, an illness or a birthday.

Should we track individually or together?

Fill it in separately, then compare together. Answering side by side tends to make answers diplomatic, which hides the very gaps you are looking for.

What if my partner refuses to track anything?

Start with the open-loop audit on your own and share one specific finding, not the whole list. A single concrete item, such as "I have booked every dentist appointment for three years", is easier to act on than a spreadsheet.
