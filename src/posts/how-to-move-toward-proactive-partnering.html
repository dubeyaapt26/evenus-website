"I shouldn't have to ask" means that asking is itself work: noticing the job, deciding it needs doing and choosing the moment to raise it. Proactive partnering moves that noticing to the person who owns the job. It is built, not wished for, through clearly owned areas, cues that prompt the owner without a reminder from their partner, and a handover the other person genuinely stops checking. It is a skill and an arrangement, not a personality type.

This article is the practical side: how the noticing actually gets built, for the partner learning it and the partner letting it go.

Why is "I shouldn't have to ask" so common?

Because asking keeps the hardest part of the job with the person who asks. To ask "can you empty the bins?", you first had to notice they were full, remember it is collection day tomorrow, and decide this is the moment to say something. The emptying takes two minutes. The rest has been running in the background all day.

The French cartoonist Emma's 2017 comic "You Should've Asked" made this point so clearly that it popularised the term "mental load". The partner who says "you should have asked" usually means it kindly. The trouble is that "just ask me" turns the asker into the household manager, permanently. Why asking for help is part of the problem goes into that dynamic in more depth.

What is the difference between reactive and proactive partnering?

A reactive partner responds to requests. A proactive partner owns a part of the household well enough that requests are not needed.

Milk runs lowBuys it when toldNotices on Tuesday and adds it to the shop

Car service dueBooks it when remindedHas a reminder set a month before, books it

Most reactive partners are not choosing to be reactive. They have never owned the area, so the cues that would trigger them have never been theirs to watch. How do you become more proactive at home?

Replace the partner as the cue. At the moment, the partner who asks is the trigger. Proactive partnering means putting a different trigger in their place.

1. Own a small number of areas fully

Two or three areas you own completely beat ten you help with. Start with one where the cues are obvious: bins, food shopping, laundry.

2. Build your own cues

Put recurring dates in your calendar yourself. Set a reminder for the day before bin collection. Keep a running shopping list on your phone and check the fridge when you make coffee. The aim is that the information reaches you directly, not via your partner.

3. Do a weekly "what's coming" scan

Five minutes on Sunday: look at the week's calendar for your areas and ask what each event needs. A party needs a present. A school trip needs a form and money. A visitor needs a bed made up. This is what anticipating feels like from the inside.

4. Close the loop out loud

When you have handled something, say so briefly: "Car's booked for the 14th." This is not seeking praise. It tells your partner they can stop holding it.

If executive function makes "notice it yourself" harder, as it can with ADHD, the same principle applies with stronger external structures: visible lists, fixed routines, alarms. That is a design question, not a commitment question. ADHD and autism in couples covers systems built for that.

How do you let go, if you are the one who always asks?

Stop being the cue, even when it is quicker to be the cue. This is the half that gets left out, and it is genuinely hard.

  • Hand over the whole area once. Explain what it involves and where the information lives, then step back.
  • Do not remind. If the bins get missed one week, the owner deals with the consequence. Reminding "just this once" restores the old arrangement.
  • Accept a different method. The shopping will contain different brands. Bedtime will run in a different order. That is what a transfer looks like.
  • Notice when it works. A short thank-you for something done without asking lands well, because it shows the difference was seen.

Here is how that looked for Priya and Tom. Tom took over food in March: planning, shopping, weekday cooking. In week two he forgot the online order and they had pasta three nights running. Priya said nothing and did not step in. By week four Tom had a recurring Thursday reminder and a list on his phone, and Priya had stopped opening the fridge to check. Four weeks of slightly odd dinners bought a permanent shift.

How do you talk about it without it becoming an argument?

Describe the arrangement, propose a specific transfer, and leave character out of it. "I'm the one who notices almost everything at home, and I'd like to hand over the food completely, planning included" opens a conversation. "You never notice anything" closes one.

Common questions

Is it reasonable to expect a partner to just know what needs doing?

For areas they own, yes, once there has been a proper handover. For areas they have never owned, expecting them to notice without the information or cues is setting both of you up for frustration.

What if my partner forgets after taking over an area?

Expect a few misses in the first month. Let the owner handle them and build their own reminders; stepping back in tends to return the area to you.

How do I stop nagging my partner?

Nagging usually means you are still the cue for jobs your partner is meant to own. Move whole areas, including the remembering, and agree that you will not remind.

Is weaponised incompetence the same as not being proactive?

No. Weaponised incompetence describes doing a task badly so it gets taken away. Not being proactive usually means never having owned the area. The fix for the second is a clear handover; the first needs an honest conversation.
