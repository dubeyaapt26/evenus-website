To measure fairness in a relationship, look at three things separately: how many hours of genuinely free time each of you has left in a week, how much personal money each of you has left after shared costs, and who holds the planning and remembering for each area of home life. Fair does not mean identical. It means neither of you is quietly giving up far more than the other.

You can do all three on paper in about half an hour. Here is the method, with a worked example and the mistakes that make the result misleading.

Why not just count chores?

Because a chore count ignores paid work, treats a five-minute job and a three-week project as equal, and misses the thinking entirely.

If one of you works fifty paid hours and the other works twenty, an equal chore list is not fair. If one of you takes the bins out and the other organises every child's birthday, a count of one each says nothing useful. And the work that causes most resentment, noticing what needs doing before anyone asks, never makes it onto a list at all. That is the mental load, and any honest measure has to include it.

Measure 1: How much free time does each of you have?

Start from the 168 hours in a week, take out everything committed, and compare what is left. Those remaining hours are your discretionary time: the hours that are genuinely yours.

  1. Start with 168.
  2. Subtract sleep. Eight hours a night is 56.
  3. Subtract basic personal time: eating, washing, getting dressed. Two hours a day is 14. That leaves 98 hours.
  4. Subtract paid work, including the hours you actually work, not your contract.
  5. Subtract commuting.
  6. Subtract household work: cooking, cleaning, childcare, admin, errands. Estimate a normal week. Do not time yourselves.

Three hours apart. On time alone, this household looks close to fair, because Ben's longer job and commute roughly balance Leila's larger share of the housework. That is a real and legitimate arrangement, and a chore count would have missed it completely.

One more check: are both totals reasonable? Two people with twelve free hours each are perfectly balanced and in trouble. If both of you are low, the answer is less total work, through outsourcing, lowering a standard or dropping something, not a redistribution.

Measure 2: Who has money left over?

Compare what each of you pays towards shared costs with your share of income, and then look at what each of you has left. The second number usually matters more.

Leila takes home £2,400 a month and Ben £3,600, so Leila earns 40% of the total and Ben 60%. Shared costs are £3,000, and they split them evenly: £1,500 each.

  • By income share, Leila would pay £1,200 and Ben £1,800. Leila is paying £300 a month more than her income share.
  • After shared costs, Leila has £900 left and Ben has £2,100.

An even split here is a choice plenty of couples make deliberately, and it is not wrong. But it is worth both of you seeing that it leaves one person with less than half the personal money of the other. Keep money on its own line. Do not convert housework into pounds to offset it against bills. Agreeing an hourly rate for your partner's time turns every task into a transaction, and it hides the thing you are trying to see. Why fair doesn't always mean equal goes into this.

Measure 3: Who holds the planning?

List the areas of home life, then each of you privately writes down who you think owns each one: me, shared, my partner, or someone we pay. Compare only when both lists are finished.

Useful areas: meal planning, groceries, home repairs, bills and renewals, the social calendar, gifts and birthdays, travel, paperwork, children's school admin, children's health, pets, and caring for parents. "Owns" means the person who notices, remembers and decides, not just the one who does the task when asked.

Leila marks eight of ten areas as hers. Ben marks four as Leila's, four as shared, and two as his. Neither is lying. This gap in perception is one of the most useful findings in the whole exercise, because it shows where the planning is invisible to one of you. Ben genuinely sees himself as sharing meal planning because he cooks twice a week. Leila sees that she decides what those two meals are, checks what is in the fridge, and writes the list.

Do not try to settle who is right. Talk about one area where you disagree, and what "shared" would actually look like there.

Should you combine it into one fairness score?

No. Keep the three measures separate. Averaging them hides exactly what you need to see.

Leila and Ben show why. Time is close. Money leaves Leila with much less of her own. Planning sits mostly with Leila, and Ben does not fully see it. A single blended number might read as "mostly fine", when the honest summary is "fine on hours, uneven on money and planning". Two separate imbalances in the same direction are a very different situation from a deliberate trade, where one partner gives more time and the other more money and both are happy with it.

What to do with the result

Pick one thing to change, try it for two weeks, then look again. Not a full renegotiation.

  • If time is uneven, move one specific recurring task, ideally the whole task including the planning, from the person with fewer free hours.
  • If money is uneven, try one month on a different split method and compare what each of you has left.
  • If the planning is uneven, hand over one whole area, from noticing to doing, rather than individual jobs.

Frame it as the system, not the person. "Most of the planning sits with one of us right now" is a conversation. "You never plan anything" is an argument. Having the talk without blame or guilt covers how to open it.

Common questions

What does a fair relationship look like?

Both partners have a similar amount of free time, both have some money of their own, and the planning is not quietly sitting with one person. The exact split can be unequal, as long as both of you chose it.

How often should couples check fairness?

Measure properly once, then check lightly every few weeks and again after any big change: a new job, a baby, a move, or illness. Arrangements drift without anyone deciding they should.

What if my partner disagrees with the numbers?

That disagreement is useful information. Different estimates usually mean one of you cannot see part of the work, so talk about the specific task where your numbers differ most.

Is emotional labour part of fairness?

It matters, but it is very hard to measure without making it feel transactional. It is better discussed than scored.
