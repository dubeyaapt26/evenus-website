To split bills as a couple, do three things in order: agree which costs are shared, choose a split method (equal, or in proportion to income), and decide how the money actually moves, through a joint bills account or transfers between you. Then settle up on the same day each month so nobody carries a running tab. Most arguments about bills come from skipping one of those three steps, usually the first.

This is the practical version: the decisions, the plumbing and a month of real-looking numbers. If your incomes are quite different, the method choice deserves more time, and splitting bills when you earn different amounts runs all four methods side by side.

Which bills should a couple split?

Split anything the household consumes, and keep separate anything one person consumes. That rule settles most items in seconds. The rest are grey areas that need a decision once, written down.

Almost always shared

  • Rent or mortgage
  • Council tax or property tax
  • Energy, water and broadband
  • Groceries and household supplies (cleaning products, loo roll, bin bags)
  • Contents or buildings insurance
  • Streaming and other subscriptions you both use

Grey areas to decide together

  • Cars and commuting. Shared if the car is used for the household's shopping and school runs, personal if it mainly gets one person to work.
  • Phones. Usually personal.
  • Eating out and takeaways. Some couples treat these as shared food, others as personal treats.
  • Pets and children. Shared, but agree whether that includes things like after-school clubs.
  • Gifts for family. Shared when they come from both of you, personal when they do not.
  • Debts brought into the relationship. Usually stay with the person who took them on, unless you choose otherwise.

The list matters more than people expect. "We split everything 50/50" means nothing until you agree what "everything" covers, and the person who quietly buys the household's milk, birthday cards and lightbulbs can end up paying a lot more than half without anyone noticing.

Which split method should you use?

Split equally if your take-home pay is close. If there is a real gap, split in proportion to income. Those two cover most couples. Two further methods exist for bigger gaps or tighter budgets.

MethodHow it worksBest for
EqualEach pays halfIncomes within roughly 15% of each other
ProportionalEach pays the same share of their incomeA real income gap, both comfortable
Equal remainderEach keeps a fixed personal allowance first, then splits the rest proportionallyOne partner earns much less or is on leave
ResidualSplit so you both end up with the same amount left overFully merged finances

The test that settles most disagreements is to look at what each of you has left after the bills, not at what each of you paid. How do couples actually pay shared bills?

There are three common set-ups: a joint bills account, separate accounts with a monthly settle-up, or fully merged money. Any split method works with any of them. Pick the one that matches how much admin you are willing to do.

1. A joint account just for bills

Each of you sets up a standing order into a shared account on payday, sized to your share. Every direct debit comes out of that account. Groceries go on the joint card. This is the lowest-effort option once it is running, and it keeps personal accounts genuinely personal. The yours, mine and ours strategy covers how to size each pot.

2. Separate accounts and transfers

Bills stay in whoever's name they are in. One person pays the rent, the other pays the utilities, both buy groceries, and once a month you add it up and one person sends the difference. This suits couples who are not ready for a joint account, renters early in a relationship and anyone who dislikes paperwork with banks. Managing household finances without a joint account goes into the details.

3. Everything merged

All income lands in one place and all spending comes from it. The split question disappears, but a different one appears: how much personal spending each person can do without discussion. Merged couples still need that number agreed.

A worked example: Priya and Tom's month

Here is a full month settled up with separate accounts and a proportional split. Priya takes home £3,000 a month and Tom £2,000, so together they bring in £5,000. Priya's share is 3,000 ÷ 5,000 = 60%, and Tom's is 40%.

Their shared bills for October:

BillAmountPaid by
Rent£1,300Priya
Council tax£150Priya
Energy£120Tom
Broadband£35Tom
Water£45Tom
Contents insurance£20Tom
Groceries£470Tom £300, Priya £170
Total£2,140

Step by step:

  1. What each should pay. Priya: 60% of £2,140 = £1,284. Tom: 40% of £2,140 = £856.
  2. What each did pay. Priya: £1,300 + £150 + £170 = £1,620. Tom: £120 + £35 + £45 + £20 + £300 = £520.
  3. The difference. Priya paid £1,620 − £1,284 = £336 more than her share. Tom paid £856 − £520 = £336 less than his.
  4. Settle up. Tom transfers £336 to Priya. Done.

The two differences always match, which is a useful check on your arithmetic. On an equal split, each share would have been £1,070, and Tom would have sent £550 instead.

If they used a joint bills account instead, Priya would pay in £1,272 and Tom £848 each month (60% and 40% of a £2,120 budget), and the extra £20 on groceries would come out of a small buffer they keep in the account.

How often should you settle up?

Once a month, on a fixed day, is the right rhythm for most couples. Weekly is more admin than it is worth. Quarterly lets the balance grow large enough that one person starts to feel like a lender.

Pick a day just after you are both paid, keep it short, and keep it about the numbers. A settle-up that turns into a review of each other's spending choices is no longer a settle-up. How to talk about money without fighting has ways to keep that conversation calm.

Tracking is what makes settling up quick. A shared note or spreadsheet works if one of you keeps it up, but notice who that is. Keeping the record is a small, repeating job, and it usually lands on the person who cares most about it being right. A checklist you can do tonight

  1. Write down every shared cost and its monthly amount.
  2. Mark the grey areas and decide each one.
  3. Compare your take-home pay and pick equal or proportional.
  4. Choose your plumbing: joint bills account, transfers, or merged.
  5. Agree a settle-up day.
  6. Agree what triggers a review: a pay rise, a job loss, a baby, a move.

Write the decisions down in one place. In a year you will want to revisit a choice you made on purpose, not argue about one nobody remembers making.

Common questions

Should the person who earns more pay more of the bills?

If the gap is large, usually yes, in proportion to income, so you both pay the same share of what you earn. If your incomes are close, an equal split is simpler and fair enough.

Do unmarried couples split bills differently?

The methods are the same. Unmarried couples often prefer separate accounts with transfers, and it is sensible to be clear whose name each bill and tenancy is in.

What if one of us pays for groceries far more often?

Count groceries as a shared bill and include them in the monthly settle-up. Frequent small purchases are the most common source of quiet imbalance.

Should childcare be split like other bills?

Most couples treat it as a shared household cost and split it with the same method as everything else. Some include it in the proportional split even when they split other bills equally, because it is often the largest single cost.
