For most unmarried couples, the right tool is whichever one gives both of you a clear, shared record of who paid what, while keeping your finances separate. A bill-splitting app such as Splitwise does that well for everyday costs. A couples money app such as Honeydue suits partners who want a joint view of bills. A shared spreadsheet or a joint bills account works too. The best choice depends on how separate you want your money to stay.

Being unmarried changes the question slightly. You are more likely to keep separate accounts, more likely to have moved in recently, and in many places you have fewer automatic legal protections than a married couple. A good record matters more, not less.

What should unmarried couples look for in an expense tracker?

Four things: a running balance, a history you can export, support for unequal splits, and as little admin as possible.

  • A running balance. At any moment, both of you can see who is ahead and by how much.
  • A durable history. Who paid the deposit, who bought the sofa, who covered rent the month one of you was between jobs. Being able to export it is worth a lot if circumstances change.
  • Unequal splits. If one of you earns noticeably more, an even split on every bill is rarely what either of you would choose on reflection. Splitting bills when you earn different amounts compares the options.
  • Low maintenance, shared. A tool only one person updates becomes that person's job. Both of you should be able to add an expense in seconds.

Bill-splitting apps: Splitwise

Splitwise is built for keeping a running tally between people who keep their own money, which makes it a natural fit for many unmarried couples. One of you adds an expense, says who paid and how to split it, and the app keeps a balance until you settle up.

Its strength is that it is not couple-specific. If you also share costs with flatmates, family or friends on holiday, it all lives in one place. Its limit is the flip side: it counts money and nothing else. If one of you pays more and the other does most of the cooking, a bill-splitting app will show the first half of that and not the second.

Couples money apps: Honeydue

Honeydue is designed for couples rather than groups, and is aimed at giving both partners a shared view of bills and spending. It suits couples who are moving towards more combined finances and want to see the household's money in one place.

Before choosing any app that connects to bank accounts, talk about how much visibility each of you actually wants. Agreeing on shared costs is one thing. Seeing every personal purchase is another, and many couples who keep separate finances do so on purpose. EvenUS vs Honeydue vs Flatastic compares the approaches in more depth.

Spreadsheets and bills accounts

A shared spreadsheet is free and endlessly flexible. A joint bills account removes most tracking altogether.

The bills-account approach is the lowest-effort option going. Both of you pay a fixed amount into one account on payday, the rent and utilities come out of it, and everything else stays separate. Maya and Jordan, for example, earn £2,800 and £1,900 a month and their fixed bills are £1,880. Splitting by income, Maya contributes 2,800 ÷ 4,700 of it, about £1,120, and Jordan about £760. Their standing orders do the rest, and they only track the irregular costs. The yours, mine and ours account strategy sets this up step by step, and managing household finances without a joint account covers couples who would rather not open one.

A spreadsheet fills the gaps: the sofa, the holiday deposit, the boiler repair. It works as long as both of you update it. Spreadsheet vs app covers where sheets work and where they tend to stall.

Where EvenUS fits

EvenUS tracks shared bills and settle-ups like an expense app, but it also measures how housework time and mental load are shared, on a separate axis. Bills can be split equally, by income (the default), so you both end up with the same left over, or with a personal floor protected first. Money is never converted into hours.

That is useful if your disagreements are not purely about money, which for many couples who have just moved in together they are not. It is less useful if you only want a tally, in which case a bill-splitting app is simpler and the better pick. EvenUS is also in closed testing and not yet on the app stores, so for now the others are the ones you can use today.

Do unmarried couples need anything besides an app?

Often, yes: a written record of what you have agreed, and possibly a cohabitation agreement.

In many countries, couples who live together without marrying or entering a civil partnership have fewer automatic rights to each other's property and money if they separate, or if one partner dies. The details vary a great deal between countries and regions, so do not assume the rules where you live match anything you have read online.

Two practical steps:

  1. Write down the big contributions. Who paid the deposit, who owns which large items, how the rent or mortgage is split. An expense app's export helps, but a short dated note both of you agree to is clearer.
  2. Consider a cohabitation agreement. This is a written agreement covering how property, savings and costs are handled, and what happens if you part. For anything involving property, children or significant sums, speak to a qualified solicitor or legal adviser where you live. This article is not legal advice.

It can feel unromantic to raise. It usually goes better framed as looking after each other rather than protecting yourself, and on a quiet weekend rather than the week a big bill is due. How to talk about money without fighting has a structure for that conversation.

A quick way to choose

  • You keep money separate and want a simple tally: a bill-splitting app.
  • You are combining finances and want one view: a couples money app.
  • Your costs are mostly fixed: a joint bills account, with a spreadsheet for extras.
  • The arguments are about the whole load, money included: a tool that measures time as well.

Common questions

Should unmarried couples have a joint bank account?

It is optional. Many couples use a joint account only for shared bills and keep everything else separate, which gives convenience without merging all their money.

What is the fairest way for unmarried couples to split rent?

There is no single answer. Equal works when incomes are close, and an income-based split is common when they differ. What matters most is that you both choose the method and write it down.

Is Splitwise good for couples?

Yes, especially for couples who keep separate finances and want a clear running balance. It tracks money only, so it will not show how housework or planning is shared.

What is a cohabitation agreement?

A written agreement between partners who live together, setting out how property, money and costs are handled during the relationship and if it ends. A qualified legal adviser can tell you how it works where you live.
