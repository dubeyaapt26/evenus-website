When work hours and incomes differ, divide chores by time and bills by money, and keep the two separate. For chores, aim for both partners to end the week with a similar number of genuinely free hours, after sleep, paid work, commuting and housework. For bills, use a method based on income. Never let a bigger salary buy someone out of housework. Mixing the two is where most of the resentment starts.

Couples get stuck because they try to settle everything on one scale. "I earn more, so I do less at home." "I work longer hours, so the house is yours." Each sounds reasonable alone. Together they produce a system in which nobody can say what fair would look like, so the argument never ends.

Why should time and money be separate?

Because they measure different things, and converting one into the other means putting a price on your partner's time. Once housework has an hourly rate, every chore becomes a transaction, and the person doing more of it is told, in effect, what their evening is worth.

Separate axes avoid that. Money answers one question: after shared costs, does each person have a fair amount left? Time answers another: after all the work, paid and unpaid, does each person have a fair number of hours that are their own? A household can be fair on one and unfair on the other, and you only see that if you look at both. How to split bills when you earn different amounts covers the money side. This article is about the time side.

How do you work out a fair split of chores?

Count each person's free time, not their chores. Start with the 168 hours in a week and subtract sleep, paid work, commuting and household work. What is left is discretionary time. Fair means you each end up with roughly the same amount.

This is how the EvenUS fairness score works, and you can do it on paper in ten minutes.

A worked example: Sam and Alex

Sam works 45 hours a week in an office and commutes 5 hours. Alex is a freelance consultant who works 32 hours from home and travels to clients for about 3 hours a week. Both sleep around 56 hours. Their household work, including planning time, comes to about 28 hours a week.

  1. Sam before housework: 168 minus 56 sleep, minus 50 work and commute, leaves 62 hours.
  2. Alex before housework: 168 minus 56 sleep, minus 35 work and travel, leaves 77 hours.
  3. Combined: 62 plus 77 is 139 hours. Take away the 28 hours of housework and 111 hours remain.
  4. Equal share: 111 divided by 2 is 55.5 hours each.
  5. So: Sam does about 6.5 hours of housework a week and Alex about 21.5.

Now the twist. Alex earns £4,000 a month and Sam £2,600. On money, Alex pays the larger share of the bills in proportion to income. On time, Alex also does the larger share of the housework, because Alex has more hours. That can feel like being asked twice. It is not. Each axis is fair on its own terms, and at the end Alex has the same free time as Sam and more money left over. Letting the salary cancel out the chores would leave Sam with far fewer free hours and nothing to show for it.

Does the mental load change the maths?

Yes. Planning work weighs more than its clock time suggests, so count it with extra weight. In the example, Alex owns meal planning and the weekly shop. The shop takes 90 minutes. The planning takes half an hour at the table, plus a week of noticing what is running low, remembering who is out on Thursday, and deciding what to cook when the plan falls through.

Sociologist Allison Daminger described this cognitive labour as anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding and monitoring. It is the part that stays invisible in a simple list of hours. If the partner at home more also ends up holding all the planning, their real load is heavier than the hours show. Being home more does not mean having more spare attention. What the mental load is goes through the examples.

A practical rule: whoever does fewer hours of housework should own at least one area outright, including its planning. Sam, at 6.5 hours, could own bins, recycling and all household admin, which is low in hours but high in remembering. That shares the noticing, not only the doing.

What about shift work and irregular hours?

Average over four weeks, not one. A nurse on three long shifts one week and five the next will look overloaded or underused depending on which week you pick. Add up four weeks of paid hours, divide by four, and plan housework around that average.

Then give the irregular worker the tasks that can move: batch cooking, the big weekly clean, admin. The partner with steady hours takes the fixed daily jobs, like school runs and evening dishes, that cannot wait for a day off.

Should the higher earner pay for a cleaner instead?

Paying for help is a good idea, but it belongs on the money axis as a shared cost, not as one person's buy-out. If a cleaner costs £60 a week, split it by the same method as your other bills. The hours it saves come off the household total, which reduces housework for both of you.

The version to avoid is "I pay for the cleaner, so the rest is yours." That turns a shared cost into a private trade, and it usually leaves the planning (booking, letting them in, deciding what they do) with the person who was supposed to be relieved.

When should you redo the split?

Whenever either person's hours change by more than a few a week. New jobs, promotions, redundancy, parental leave and a return to study all change the free-hours sum. A split agreed two years ago is history, not a rule. Rebalancing after a job loss or promotion covers what to change first.

Common questions

Is it fair for the person who works part-time to do all the housework?

Not all of it, usually. Run the free-hours sum: in most cases the part-time partner does more, but not everything, and the full-time partner still owns some areas and their planning.

Does a longer commute count as work?

For free time, yes. Commuting hours are not discretionary, so subtract them just like paid work. An hour on a train each way is ten hours a week that neither person can spend on housework or rest.

What if we disagree about how long chores take?

Estimate each one separately, then compare. Gaps in the estimates are useful information, because they usually point to hidden steps one person does and the other has not noticed.

Should we count childcare as a chore?

Count it as household work in the time sum. Leaving it out is how a parent at home ends up recorded as having endless free time.
