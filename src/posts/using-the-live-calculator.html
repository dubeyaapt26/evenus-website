To use the relationship balance calculator, open the free household fairness calculator and move three pairs of sliders, one for each partner: monthly income, paid work hours a week, and housework and planning hours a week. It instantly shows how many free hours each of you has left, the gap between you, how shared costs would split by income, and one suggested change. No account is needed and nothing is stored.

It takes about two minutes. The harder part is choosing honest numbers, so most of this guide is about what to enter and how to read what comes back.

What does the relationship balance calculator measure?

Free time first, money second. It works out how many hours a week each of you has that are genuinely your own, and separately shows how shared costs would be divided in proportion to income.

The starting point is the 168 hours in a week. The calculator takes out sleep and the personal time nobody has a choice about, such as eating and getting ready, which leaves roughly 68 hours to allocate. From those it subtracts each person's paid work and housework. What remains is discretionary time. A fair week, by this measure, is one where both of you end up with a similar number of free hours.

Money is shown alongside but never converted into hours. The calculator does not put a price on housework.

What do you need to enter?

Three numbers for each partner. The two partners are labelled Alex and Sam; just decide which of you is which.

Paid work, hours a week0 to 70Contracted hours plus regular overtime. Add your commute here too, since there is no separate commute slider.

How to estimate housework hours honestly

This is the slider people get wrong most often, usually by forgetting the thinking. A quick way to estimate it:

  1. List your daily tasks and multiply by seven. Thirty minutes of cooking every day is 3.5 hours.
  2. Add weekly tasks: the big shop, the bathroom, the laundry.
  3. Add the planning you do: writing lists, booking, keeping track of dates. Half an hour here and there adds up.
  4. Round to the nearest hour. Close enough is fine.

Fill it in separately first, then compare. If you each guess very different numbers for the same person, that difference is worth talking about in itself. The invisible labour checklist helps with the planning tasks people tend to forget.

How do you read the result?

Look at the free hours and the gap first, then the suggestion. The fairness number is a summary, not a grade.

  • Free hours. Shown for each partner as bars, with a headline such as "Alex has 7 more free hours."
  • Fairness score. A single number out of 100 that drops as the gap between your free hours grows.
  • Shared costs. Each person's percentage of shared costs if you split in proportion to income.
  • This week's one thing. A suggested change sized to the gap. If you are within a couple of hours of each other, it suggests nothing at all.

A worked example with real numbers

The calculator opens on an example household, which is a useful way to see the arithmetic.

Alex earns 4,500 a month, works 42 hours, and does 8 hours of housework and planning. Sam earns 4,200, works 34 hours, and does 23 hours at home.

  • Alex: 68 − 42 − 8 = 18 free hours
  • Sam: 68 − 34 − 23 = 11 free hours
  • Gap: 18 − 11 = 7 hours a week
  • Shared costs by income: 4,500 ÷ 8,700 is about 52% for Alex, 48% for Sam

The fairness number comes out at 87, and the suggestion is for Alex to take Thursday's dinner and washing up, about two hours that close most of the gap without touching the weekend.

Notice what this shows. Alex works eight more paid hours, so a task count might say Alex is carrying more. Sam still ends up with seven fewer free hours because of the work at home. That is the kind of picture the calculator is built to reveal.

Now try a variation. If Sam moves to 30 paid hours and Alex picks up 4 more hours at home, Alex has 68 − 42 − 12 = 14 and Sam has 68 − 30 − 23 = 15. The gap is one hour, and the calculator suggests nothing. Even, for now.

What doesn't the calculator capture?

It is a simplified version of the EvenUS model, and it says so. Know its limits before you treat the result as fact.

  • Mental load is folded in, not weighted. Planning goes in the same slider as physical work. The app gives planning-heavy tasks extra weight. The calculator does not.
  • Only one split method. It shows a proportional split. There are other fair methods, compared in how to split bills when you earn different amounts.
  • Balance is not the same as wellbeing. Two people with four free hours each look even and are both stretched. A small gap is not automatically good news.
  • One week, not a pattern. A busy month at work can skew it. Try it with typical numbers, not this week's worst.

What should you do with the result?

Use it to start one specific conversation, not to settle who is right.

If the gap is small, there may be nothing to change. If it is large, pick one task to move, ideally a whole area including the planning, and try it for two weeks. Common questions

Is the calculator free?

Yes. It runs in your browser, needs no account or email, and stores nothing.

Can I use pounds or rupees?

The sliders display dollars, but the income split is a ratio, so it works the same whatever currency you earn in. Enter your monthly take-home in the same units for both of you.

Why is there no commute slider?

To keep it to three inputs. Add your commuting time to paid work hours to get the same effect.

What score counts as fair?

There is no pass mark. Look at the gap in hours instead: within a couple of hours is close to even, and anything larger is worth a conversation.
