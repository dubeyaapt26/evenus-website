To divide household chores fairly without a fight, group the jobs into whole areas (meals, laundry, kids' admin), estimate each area's weekly time and how much planning it involves, then split the areas so that both of you end up with a similar amount of free time once paid work is counted. Settle it in one calm, planned session, and let each person run their areas their own way. Most fights start when chores are divided task by task, in the moment, with no shared idea of what fair means.

This is the method for the dividing itself. If you are not yet at the point where you can sit down together, start with how to talk to your partner about splitting chores.

Why does dividing chores so often start a fight?

Because each person is usually comparing a different list. One of you counts the jobs you can see being done. The other counts those jobs plus all the planning around them. Both lists are honest, and they do not match.

Three other things reliably turn the conversation into an argument:

  • Doing it in the moment. Dividing chores while the sink is full means one of you is already annoyed.
  • Splitting tasks, not areas. Thirty separate tasks means thirty small negotiations.
  • No agreed definition of fair. If one of you means "equal hours" and the other means "equal free time", you will both be right and still disagree.

What is a fair way to split household chores?

A fair split leaves both of you with a similar number of hours that are genuinely your own each week. That means counting paid work and commuting alongside housework, and counting the planning, not only the doing.

So a 50/50 split of chores is only fair when your paid workloads are similar. If one of you works 30 hours and the other 45, the person working fewer paid hours taking on more housework can be entirely fair. How to divide housework without it becoming an argument explains why the remembering matters as much as the hours.

How to divide chores step by step

Set aside an hour, at a time when neither of you is tired or rushing. Bring a sheet of paper or a shared note.

1. Group the chores into areas

Write every recurring job, then cluster them into areas someone can own from start to finish. Typical areas: meals, food shopping, laundry, cleaning, children's admin, bills and paperwork, outside jobs and repairs, family and social life (birthdays, presents, visits).

2. Estimate the weekly time for each area

Rough estimates are fine. Agree the number together, and where you disagree, use the lower one for now.

3. Mark the planning-heavy areas

Some areas are cheap in hours but expensive in attention. Children's admin might be three hours of doing and a whole week of noticing emails, forms and dates. Mark these, because they weigh more than their hours.

4. Work out each person's target

Start from paid work and commute. Whoever has fewer hours there can take on more at home, so that the totals come out close.

5. Pick areas in turns

Take it in turns to choose an area, starting with whoever has the larger target. People pick what they mind least, which tends to produce a split you both actually like.

6. Adjust for planning and preferences

Look at the result. If one person has ended up with every planning-heavy area, move one across even if the hours shift slightly.

A worked example

Kiran works 40 hours a week and Leo works 30. Their commutes are similar. Their household areas add up to 24.5 hours a week.

For their total working week to come out equal, Leo needs to do 10 more hours at home than Kiran, to match Kiran's extra 10 hours of paid work. With 24.5 hours to share: Kiran takes about 7.25 and Leo about 17.25. Check: 40 + 7.25 = 47.25, and 30 + 17.25 = 47.25.

Their first draft gives Leo meals, laundry, children's admin and shopping (17 hours) and Kiran cleaning, outside jobs and bills (7.5 hours). Then they look at step 6. Leo now holds both of the planning-heavy areas. So they swap: Kiran takes children's admin (3 hours, lots of noticing) and Leo takes cleaning (4 hours, very little). Leo is now on 18 hours and Kiran on 6.5, so their working weeks come to 48 and 46.5. The hours are no longer perfectly matched, but the planning is shared, which matters more than an hour and a half, and both say it feels fairer.

How do you keep the conversation calm?

Agree a few ground rules at the start and stick to the paper. A shared sheet in front of you shifts the focus from each other to the arrangement.

  • Talk about the future split, not about who did what last year.
  • No comments on how the other person does an area they own.
  • If it gets heated, stop and pick it up another day. A half-finished split is better than a finished row.
  • Anything you cannot agree on goes on a "try for a month" list.

Different standards are the most common sticking point. Agree what has to happen for each area to count as done, and leave the rest to the owner. "Bathroom cleaned weekly" is a shared standard. "Towels folded in thirds" is a preference.

What if nobody wants a particular chore?

You have three choices: rotate it, pay for it, or do less of it. All three are legitimate.

  • Rotate: swap the job monthly. This works for physical jobs with no planning.
  • Pay for it: a cleaner, a laundry service or a meal kit, if the budget allows. Decide together how it is paid for.
  • Lower the bar: ironing, elaborate weekday dinners and perfectly sorted cupboards are often optional.

How do you make the split last?

Review it briefly every week or two, and fully after any big change. A split that worked before a new job or a baby will need redoing.

Common questions

Should chores be split 50/50 if we both work full-time?

If your paid hours and commutes are similar, a roughly even split of time is a good starting point. Make sure the planning is shared too, not just the doing.

Is it better to rotate chores or assign them permanently?

Permanent ownership usually works better for areas involving planning, because the owner builds a rhythm. Rotation suits disliked physical jobs.

How do we divide chores when one of us works from home?

Working from home is still paid work. Count the hours the same way, and be careful that being at home does not quietly make one person the default for deliveries and repairs.

What chores are usually forgotten when dividing housework?

Planning meals, buying presents, booking appointments, renewing insurance and keeping in touch with family are the ones most often missed.
