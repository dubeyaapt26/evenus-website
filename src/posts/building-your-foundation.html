To save for a house together without straining your monthly budget, work out the full target (deposit plus buying costs), divide it by the months you have, and have each partner contribute in proportion to their income rather than half each. Move the money automatically on payday, keep it apart from everyday spending, and write down who put in what. The rest of this piece is the arithmetic, and the conversations that make the plan last.

No figures here are mortgage or product advice. Lending rules, purchase taxes and savings products differ by country and change often. Use the method, then take your numbers to a qualified mortgage adviser.

How much do you actually need to save?

More than the deposit. The deposit is the number everyone talks about, so it is the number couples plan around. Then the buying costs arrive and the plan falls short by a few thousand.

Build your target from these lines:

  • The deposit itself. Your adviser or lender can tell you what size of deposit is realistic for the homes you are looking at.
  • Purchase taxes and registration fees. These vary a great deal by country, region and buyer status.
  • Legal and conveyancing fees, plus any survey or valuation.
  • Lender or arrangement fees, if your mortgage has them.
  • Moving costs, and the things a new home needs on day one: curtains, a bed frame, a fridge if one is not included.
  • A separate emergency fund. Not part of the house money. If the boiler breaks a week after you move in, you do not want to be paying for it on a credit card.

Get rough figures for each line, add them up, and that is your target. A deposit target with no buying costs is the most common reason a house plan stretches by an extra year.

A worked example: Priya and Tom

Priya takes home £3,300 a month and Tom takes home £2,200. Together that is £5,500. Rent, bills and food come to £2,500, which they already split in proportion to income: Priya pays £1,500 (60%) and Tom pays £1,000 (40%). That leaves Priya with £1,800 and Tom with £1,200.

Their full target, deposit plus costs, is £24,000. They want to buy in three years.

  1. Monthly amount: £24,000 divided by 36 months is £667 a month.
  2. Split it proportionally: 60% of £667 is £400 for Priya, and 40% is £267 for Tom.
  3. Check what is left: Priya has £1,400 a month for herself and Tom has £933.

Now compare a 50/50 split of the saving: £333.50 each. Priya would keep £1,466.50 and Tom £866.50. That gap is wider than it was before they started saving. Tom is giving up 28% of his free money for the house, and Priya 19%. Splitting the house fund the same way you split the bills keeps the sacrifice even, which is what stops one person quietly resenting the plan by month eight.

What if the monthly figure is too high?

Change the timeline before you squeeze the budget. A plan you can keep up for 48 months will get you there sooner than one you abandon after nine.

If £667 a month leaves either of you with too little, try these in order:

  • Extend the timeline. Over 48 months, £24,000 is £500 a month: £300 and £200.
  • Lower the target. A smaller home, a different area, or a smaller deposit if your adviser thinks it is workable.
  • Agree a windfall rule. Bonuses, tax refunds and cash gifts go half to the house fund and half to whoever received them. Decide this before the money arrives, not after.
  • Cut shared costs together. Trimming a subscription or a takeaway habit you share helps both of you at once. Cutting only one person's spending turns into a negotiation.

One rule protects the whole plan: never let the personal allowance of the lower earner fall to almost nothing. A house fund that leaves one partner unable to buy a friend's birthday present without asking is not saving. It is a slow argument.

Practise the mortgage before you have one

If your future housing cost will be higher than your rent, start paying the difference into the house fund now. It is the most honest test of affordability there is.

Say Priya and Tom pay £1,300 in rent and their adviser's rough estimate puts mortgage plus ownership costs (insurance, maintenance, service charges) at £1,550. They put the extra £250 into the house fund every month from today. If they cannot live on that, they learn it now, while it costs them nothing. If they can, the extra money is already part of the deposit.

Where should you keep the house fund?

Somewhere separate from everyday spending, which you both can see. A savings account in joint names, or one per partner with a shared view of the balances, both work. What matters is that it is not the account you buy groceries from.

Which savings product to use, and whether any government scheme for first-time buyers applies to you, depends on your country and circumstances. That is a question for a qualified financial or mortgage adviser, not a blog. If you are already running separate and joint accounts, the house fund fits naturally into the yours, mine and ours structure as a fourth pot.

Then automate it. A standing order on the day after payday means the decision is made once, not every month. Automating household spending covers the payday sequence step by step.

Who owns what if you put in different amounts?

Write it down from the first month. With a proportional split, Priya will have put in £14,400 and Tom about £9,600 after three years. That is fair, and it is also a fact that matters if you buy a home together.

How a property can be jointly owned, and how unequal contributions are recognised, depends on the law where you live. If you are unmarried, the question matters even more. Talk to a solicitor or property lawyer before you buy, and keep a simple shared record of contributions until then. It is a dull spreadsheet that removes a very large future argument.

If either of you is carrying debt, deal with it in the plan rather than around it. Clearing expensive borrowing can matter as much as the deposit, and lenders will look at it. Managing debt as a couple covers that conversation, and a free debt charity can help with the numbers.

The work nobody counts: running the house hunt

Saving is the money side. The search is a mental-load side, and it usually lands on one person. Someone tracks the listings, books viewings, compares mortgage options, chases the solicitor, keeps the document folder and remembers the deadline for the survey. That is anticipating, deciding and monitoring, the cognitive labour that sociologist Allison Daminger described in her 2019 research on household work. It takes hours and it rarely gets credited.

Split it as clearly as you split the money. One person owns the finance side: adviser meetings, paperwork, tracking the fund. The other owns the search: alerts, viewings, the shortlist. Swap halfway if one side turns out heavier. What the mental load is explains why the planning matters as much as the doing.

Common questions

Should we save in a joint account or separately?

Either works if both of you can see the total and you have agreed the contributions. A joint savings account is simplest to track. Separate accounts with a shared record can suit unmarried couples who want contributions to stay clearly attributed.

What if one of us has much more already saved?

Record each person's starting amount separately from the monthly contributions. Then get legal advice on how the purchase will reflect it, so the larger starting sum is recognised rather than quietly merged.

How often should we review the plan?

Every six months, and whenever either income changes. A promotion, a job loss or parental leave should change both the monthly figure and the split.

Should we stop paying into pensions to save faster?

That trade-off has long-term consequences that depend on your age, employer contributions and country. It is exactly the kind of question to put to a qualified financial adviser before you change anything.
