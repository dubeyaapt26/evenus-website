After a job loss, a promotion or a move, re-split chores and bills on purpose rather than letting them drift. Count job hunting and relocation admin as real work, compare each partner's free hours rather than who is "at home", switch to a bill split that protects a personal allowance when one income drops, and agree a review date so the temporary arrangement does not become permanent.

Each of these events changes the two numbers that any household arrangement depends on: how much time each of you has, and how much money each of you brings in. The arrangement you had was built on the old numbers.

Should the partner who lost their job do all the housework?

No, not by default. They will usually have more time and should take on more, but looking for work is work, and the loss itself takes energy.

The common pattern goes like this. One partner is made redundant on a Thursday. By the following week the other partner is saying, kindly enough, "since you're home, could you just handle the shopping, the cleaning and the dinners?" Within a month the person at home is doing everything, job hunting in the gaps, and feeling that their time is no longer seen as valuable. The working partner, meanwhile, feels they are carrying the money alone. Both are under strain and neither caused it.

A better starting point: treat the job search as part-time work with set hours, then share the household on top of that.

The numbers are estimates, and that is fine. The point of writing them down is to replace "you're at home all day" with something you can both see.

Which jobs should move?

Move whole areas, including the planning, not single tasks. "Weekday dinners are yours for now" is cleaner than a list of favours. Areas that can be done in flexible daytime hours, such as the weekly shop, appointments and admin calls, suit the person whose schedule is now flexible. How do you split bills when one partner has no income?

Change the method as well as the amounts. A proportional split gives a partner with no income a share of zero, which can feel like losing all say. An equal split becomes impossible. The method that usually works best is equal remainder: protect a small personal allowance for each of you first.

Alex takes home £3,200 a month. Sam receives £400 a month from savings set aside for this. Shared costs are £2,300.

  • Proportional: Alex has 89% of the income, so pays about £2,044 and Sam about £256. Alex keeps £1,156 and Sam keeps £144.
  • Equal remainder with £300 protected each: that leaves £2,900 and £100 to cover £2,300. Split in proportion to those amounts, Alex pays about £2,223 and Sam about £77. Alex keeps about £977 and Sam keeps about £323.

The protected floor means Sam is not asking permission for a haircut or a coffee with a former colleague, and that matters more than the size of the sum. What changes after a promotion or a more demanding job?

The partner with more paid hours does less at home, for an agreed period, and the other partner's extra load is named rather than absorbed.

A promotion often adds hours, travel and evening calls. Without a conversation, the other partner simply starts doing more, and a year later the household has a new default nobody chose. Set it up explicitly:

  • Agree which areas move and for how long, for example "school pickups are mine until the end of the project".
  • Revisit the money split if the pay rise is large. Proportional splitting adjusts automatically. Equal splitting does not.
  • Check free hours again after the first month, when the real workload is clear.

How do you share the load after a relocation?

Treat the move as a project with named owners, because the mental load is enormous and invisible. Finding a home, schools, a GP, utilities, a bank, transferring records and working out bin days in a new place is weeks of planning and remembering.

Two things often go unnoticed. First, the partner who moved for the other's job may also be the one doing most of the settling in, while also leaving their own work, friends and routine. Second, if you have moved country, whoever speaks the language or knows the systems best becomes the household's admin department by default. Split the project by area: one person owns housing and utilities, the other owns schools and healthcare registrations. A checklist for any big change in paid work

  1. Write down each partner's paid hours, commute and household hours, before and after.
  2. Count job hunting, retraining or relocation admin as work.
  3. Compare free hours, not who is home.
  4. Move whole areas, including the planning, to close most of the gap.
  5. Choose a bill-split method that fits the new incomes.
  6. Set a review date, usually six to eight weeks away.

When it is more than a practical problem

Losing a job can hit confidence, identity and mood, and it can be hard on the partner still working too. If either of you is struggling for more than a few weeks, talking to a GP or a counsellor is a reasonable step. Rebalancing the chores is not a substitute for that support.

Common questions

How long should a temporary chore split last after a job loss?

Agree a review date of six to eight weeks, then check again. Once the new job starts, go back through the same checklist rather than assuming the old split returns.

Is it fair to stop splitting bills 50/50 after redundancy?

Yes. A 50/50 split assumes similar incomes. When one income falls sharply, a proportional or equal remainder split keeps both of you with some money of your own.

What if the partner at home feels they are being treated like staff?

That usually means tasks were handed over without ownership or without counting the job search. Move whole areas, count search hours as work, and agree the arrangement together.

Do we need to rebalance after a small pay rise?

Not usually. Revisit when paid hours, commute or income change enough that one of you notices the difference in your week.
