AI tools can genuinely help couples with the logistics of a shared life: turning a brain dump into a list, drafting a meal plan, summarising school emails, or sorting a month of spending into categories. They are much less reliable for relationship insight. A chatbot only hears one side, tends to agree with whoever is typing, and cannot see the work nobody mentions. Use AI to lighten the admin. Leave the judgement about your relationship to the two of you, and to a counsellor if you need one.

We should be upfront about our own position. EvenUS deliberately has no AI chat coach, and it does not let a large language model generate relationship advice. The rest of this article explains why, and where we think AI is useful.

Where can AI actually help couples with household balance?

It helps most with the clerical part of the mental load: capturing, organising and drafting. These are tasks where a mistake is easy to spot and cheap to fix.

Allison Daminger's 2019 research describes cognitive household labour as anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding and monitoring. AI tools are reasonably good at one of those steps, identifying options, and can help a little with capture. Here are some realistic uses:

  • Brain dump to list. Paste a messy voice note of everything in your head and ask for it grouped by area: school, health, house, money.
  • Meal planning. "Five weeknight dinners under 30 minutes, one vegetarian, no nuts, using what's left from Sunday's roast."
  • Summarising long messages. Turning a three-page school newsletter into dates and actions.
  • Drafting admin messages. A polite email to the landlord about the boiler, or a complaint to the energy company.
  • Categorising spending. Sorting an exported statement into groceries, transport, childcare and so on, before you discuss it.
  • Packing and preparation lists. Holidays, a new baby, moving house.

Each of these saves time for whoever holds that area. That matters, because the person carrying the mental load is usually short of time above all. Offloading your mental to-do list covers the non-AI version of the brain dump.

What AI does not change

A tool that writes the meal plan still leaves someone deciding to ask for one, checking it suits everyone, ordering the food and noticing on Wednesday that the plan has fallen apart. AI can make one person's load lighter. It does not move that load to the other partner. If one of you does all the prompting, you have a faster household manager, not a shared one. What the mental load is explains why that distinction matters.

Can AI give good relationship advice?

It can give generic advice that sounds reasonable, but it is a poor judge of your particular conflict. There are three structural reasons, and none of them is about the technology being primitive.

It only hears one side

Picture Sam after an argument about the washing up, typing: "My partner never helps in the kitchen and gets defensive when I bring it up. Am I being unreasonable?" The chatbot gets Sam's framing, Sam's word "never", and Sam's version of events. Alex, upstairs, could type a completely different account of the same evening and get an equally sympathetic answer. Neither reply contains any new information. Each just reflects its author's view back to them.

It tends to agree

Chat assistants are generally designed to be helpful and agreeable to the person they are talking to. In a disagreement between partners, that is the wrong instinct. What you usually need is someone who can hold both views at once, or a tool that shows where the two views differ.

It cannot see unreported work

The invisible part of household work is invisible because nobody mentions it. A chatbot that is told "I do the cooking and the bins" knows nothing about the dentist appointments, the birthday presents or the night-time worrying, unless someone says so. The tool can only be as fair as its input, and the input is usually one person's memory of one bad evening.

There is also a safety issue. Conversations about relationship conflict can touch on serious things: control, fear, mental health crises. A general chatbot may respond carelessly at exactly the wrong moment. If your relationship feels unsafe or you are struggling, speak to a qualified counsellor, your GP, or a local support service rather than a chatbot.

Why EvenUS does not use an AI coach

Because a relationship tool should rely on what both partners have recorded and on rules you can understand, not on generated advice. EvenUS measures how money, time and mental load are shared from what each partner logs. It then suggests one specific swap per week, worked out from fixed rules. It shows where you see things differently without taking a side, and it keeps each person's weekly check-in private. If things stay difficult for about two months, it stops making suggestions, says so honestly, points you toward a counsellor, and goes quiet.

None of that needs a language model writing advice, and adding one would bring in the problems above. How the EvenUS fairness score works sets out the method in full.

How should couples use AI tools together?

Agree a few ground rules, the same way you would for a shared calendar or bank account. Here is a practical set:

Good usePoor use
Drafting a meal plan you both reviewAsking who is right about last night's argument
Turning a brain dump into a shared listPasting your partner's private messages into a chatbot
Categorising a bank export before a money talkLetting a chatbot decide your bill split for you
Writing an email to a tradespersonWriting apology or conflict messages to your partner
Summarising a long school emailUsing it as a stand-in for a counsellor

Some further points worth agreeing:

  1. Privacy. Your partner's health details, finances and messages are theirs. Don't share them with a third-party tool without asking.
  2. Ownership stays with a person. If AI drafts the meal plan, the person who owns meals still owns it. The tool is an assistant to the owner, not a replacement.
  3. Check facts. AI tools can state wrong dates, prices and rules with confidence. Anything financial, legal or medical needs a proper source or a qualified professional.
  4. Talk to each other about the hard things. Use tools for the logistics so you have more time for the conversations that matter.

For the broader question of how much technology a couple needs at all, digital minimalism for couples is a useful counterweight, and shared to-do lists for couples covers the simpler tools that often do the job.

Common questions

Is it a bad idea to ask ChatGPT for relationship advice?

For general information it can be a starting point. For a specific conflict, it only hears your version, so treat its answer as a reflection of your own view rather than an outside opinion.

Can AI track who does more housework?

Only if someone tells it what was done, and it only knows what it is told. A fair picture needs both partners' input, including the planning and remembering that are easy to leave out.

Is it safe to share our finances with an AI tool?

Check the tool's privacy policy and whether your data is stored or used for training. Remove account numbers and personal details, and ask your partner before sharing anything about them.

Can AI replace couples therapy?

No. A qualified counsellor hears both of you, notices what is not said, and is accountable for their advice. A chatbot does none of those things.
