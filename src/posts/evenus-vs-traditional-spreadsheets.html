When is a spreadsheet the better choice for a couple?

When one or both of you genuinely enjoy it, your finances are simple, and money is the only thing you want to track. Spreadsheets have real strengths that no app matches.

  • They cost nothing. Google Sheets, Excel or Numbers, all of which you probably already have.
  • You own the data and the logic. Every formula is visible. Nothing is a black box.
  • They bend to odd situations. A shared car loan with a third person, a sabbatical, a cross-border household paying bills in two currencies. A spreadsheet can model any of it.
  • They work well for planning. Saving for a deposit, comparing mortgage options or mapping a year of irregular bills is spreadsheet work, and an app built for weekly tracking is not the right tool. Budgeting for couples has a template for exactly that.

If you are both happy with a shared sheet and it has run for a year without friction, keep it. Nothing in this article is a reason to switch.

Why do couples' budget spreadsheets stop working?

Usually not because of the formulas, but because maintaining the sheet becomes one more job, and it tends to land on whoever already does the household admin.

Take Sam and Alex. Sam built the sheet in January: a tab for bills, a tab for groceries, a formula splitting everything by income. For three months it is great. Then Sam is away for work for a fortnight, nobody updates it, and when Sam gets back there are eleven receipts to reconstruct on a Sunday night. Alex is happy to help but does not know how the sheet works. By May, Sam is the only person who understands it, the only one who updates it, and the only one who notices when it is wrong.

That is the mental load in miniature: the noticing, remembering and keeping-track that sits around the visible task. A spreadsheet does not create it, but it gives it one more place to collect. Three quieter limits

  • Money only. Most arguments about fairness at home are not purely about money. A sheet that shows a perfect 60/40 split says nothing about who did forty hours of housework and who did eight.
  • No private space. Every cell is visible to both of you. There is nowhere to record "this week felt heavy" without it becoming a statement to your partner.
  • It shows numbers, not a next step. A sheet will tell you there is a gap. It will not suggest what to change.

What does an app do that a spreadsheet can't?

The main difference is not automation. It is that an app can measure time and mental load alongside money, keep some answers private, and turn the result into one suggestion.

Here is what EvenUS does, stated plainly:

  • Money on its own axis. Bills split equally, by income (the default), so you both end up with the same left over, or with a personal floor protected first. It works out settle-ups and tracks them. Money is never converted into hours.
  • Time as discretionary hours. A week has 168 hours. Take out sleep, paid work, commuting and household work, and what is left is genuinely yours. Fairness means both of you end up with a similar number of those hours.
  • Mental load as hours. Planning, remembering and noticing count as time on top of the doing, rather than as a separate score or a multiplier.
  • A private weekly check-in. Two questions, readable only by the person who answered, enforced in the database rather than just hidden in the interface.
  • One swap a week. Who, what, and why now. Not a list.

What does an app not do as well?

An app gives up flexibility and costs money, and EvenUS in particular has limits worth knowing.

  • No custom formulas. If your household has an unusual arrangement the four split methods do not cover, a spreadsheet will model it better.
  • No bank connection. Bills are logged by hand. That is deliberate, but some people want automatic imports.
  • It needs both of you. It compares two people. If only one partner is interested, a spreadsheet degrades more gracefully.
  • It is not a budgeting or savings planner. It measures how the shared load is distributed. It does not forecast your pension.
  • It is not available yet. EvenUS is in closed testing. A spreadsheet can be set up tonight.

A spreadsheet setup that holds up

If you stay with a spreadsheet, build it so either of you can update it in two minutes. These columns cover most couples:

  1. Date
  2. What it was (rent, food, energy)
  3. Amount
  4. Paid by
  5. Split type (income, even, or one person)
  6. Each person's expected share, as a formula from the split type and your income shares
  7. Running difference, the total each person paid minus their total expected share

Keep the income shares in one cell each so a pay rise means one edit. Settle up on a fixed date each month. And agree in advance who updates it, alternating months if possible, so the maintenance itself is shared. If you want to know what split method to put in column five, splitting bills when you earn different amounts compares all four with the same numbers.

How to decide tonight

Ask each other, separately, one question: is our problem keeping track of money, or feeling that the whole load is uneven?

If you both say money, and you are both comfortable with spreadsheets, use a spreadsheet. If one of you says the load, money is probably not the only thing that needs measuring, and a sheet will not show the part that hurts. Common questions

Is Google Sheets good for couples' budgeting?

Yes. It is free, both of you can edit at the same time, and it works on a phone. It suits couples who are comfortable with formulas and willing to share the job of updating it.

Can a spreadsheet track chores as well as money?

It can, with a tab of tasks, owners and estimated hours. In practice chore tabs are usually abandoned within weeks, because logging housework by hand in a grid is tedious and the thinking work is hard to enter at all.

Does EvenUS connect to your bank account?

No. Bills are entered by hand. That keeps it working across banks and countries and avoids sharing account access, at the cost of some typing.
