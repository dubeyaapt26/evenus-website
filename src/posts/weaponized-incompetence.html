Weaponised incompetence (often spelled weaponized incompetence) is when someone does a household task badly, or says they cannot do it, so the job ends up back with their partner. To handle it, talk about the pattern rather than the person, hand over the whole outcome instead of single tasks, allow a learning period, and stop rescuing the task when it goes wrong. Most of the time it is a habit rather than a plan, which is why naming it calmly works better than accusing.

The phrase has become popular online without a single clear originator. It is a useful label, but it can also be used too quickly, so it helps to know what it is and what it is not.

What is weaponised incompetence?

It is a pattern where seeming unable to do something gets you out of doing it. The incompetence might be real, exaggerated or simply never addressed. What makes it "weaponised" is the effect: the task keeps returning to the same person.

Here is how it looks in the kitchen, where it shows up often.

Jordan offers to cook on Tuesday. At 6pm he asks Maya where the colander is. At 6:10 he asks what temperature the chicken needs. At 6:20 he asks whether the rice needs rinsing. Dinner is late, slightly burnt, and the kitchen looks like a storm went through it. Maya clears up. Next Tuesday, it seems easier for Maya to cook. A few weeks later, cooking is simply Maya's job, and nobody decided it.

Jordan may not have intended any of that. The result is the same either way: one person's lack of practice has quietly become the other person's workload.

What are examples of weaponised incompetence?

The common ones are small, repeated and often slightly funny the first time.

  • Shrinking a jumper in the wash, and then never doing the laundry again.
  • Buying the wrong things on the shopping trip, every time, despite a list.
  • "I don't know what the children like for lunch" after several years of the children eating lunch.
  • Asking so many questions about a task that doing it yourself is quicker.
  • Loading the dishwasher in a way that means it has to be reloaded.
  • "You're just better at this" about anything involving forms, gifts or appointments.

Any one of these can be an honest mistake. It becomes a pattern when it repeats and nothing changes.

Is it weaponised incompetence or something else?

Before you name it, rule out three other things that look similar. Getting this right changes the whole conversation.

Weaponised incompetenceRepeated poor results, no improvement, the task returns to youHand over the outcome and stop rescuing

Different standardsThe job gets done, just not to your standardAgree a minimum, then accept their way
Executive function difficultiesTrouble starting, remembering or organising tasks, often across all of lifeSystems, reminders and a different division

The second and third are very common and easy to mistake for weaponised incompetence. If the dishes are clean but stacked differently, that is a standards question, which "I don't mind the mess" deals with. If your partner has ADHD or similar, forgetting can be real and not a strategy. Why does it happen?

Usually because the system rewards it, not because anyone is scheming. If doing a task badly means it never comes back to you, the habit forms naturally, even without any intention.

The other partner often plays a part too, without meaning to. Stepping in to fix the dinner, refolding the towels or redoing the shop all teach the same lesson: someone else will catch it. This is not about blame on either side. It is a loop, and loops can be broken from either end.

It also connects to the mental load. A partner who never has to notice what needs doing never builds the knowledge to do it well. What the mental load is explains how that knowledge collects with one person.

How do you handle weaponised incompetence?

Change the structure so that doing it badly no longer moves the task back to you. Here is a step-by-step approach.

  1. Pick a calm moment and talk about the pattern. "I've noticed cooking has drifted back to me since the chicken night. I'd like to change that." Avoid the label itself in the first conversation. It tends to sound like an accusation.
  2. Hand over an outcome, not a task. Not "can you cook Tuesday?" but "Tuesday and Thursday dinners are yours, including deciding what and buying it." Ownership includes the thinking. Helping out versus taking ownership explains why this matters.
  3. Do one proper handover. Share the knowledge once: where things are, what the children will eat, the recipes that work. Write it down if it helps.
  4. Agree a learning period. Four weeks where mistakes are expected and not commented on. Skills take repetition.
  5. Stop rescuing. If dinner is late, it is late. If the wrong shopping arrives, the owner fixes it. This is the hardest step for the person who used to own the task.
  6. Review the outcome, not the method. After a month, talk about whether dinners are happening, not how the onions were chopped.

What to say if your partner says "you're just better at it"

Agree, and explain why that is the problem: "I'm better because I've done it for six years. You'll be just as good after six months." Skill comes from practice, and practice comes from ownership.

What if you recognise yourself in this?

That is a useful thing to notice, and it is easy to change. Pick one area your partner usually handles and take it over completely for a month, without asking questions you could answer yourself. Look things up, make your own decisions, and accept that you will get some of it wrong at first.

When it is more than a habit

If the pattern continues after an honest conversation and a real handover, it may be about something bigger. Sometimes it reflects a disagreement about whose job the household is, or deeper tension in the relationship. A couples counsellor is the right person to help with that. Dividing labour without blame or guilt is a good starting point for the conversation before it gets to that stage.

Common questions

Is weaponised incompetence always deliberate?

No. It is often an unexamined habit that formed because the other partner kept stepping in. The effect matters more than the intention.

Can weaponised incompetence happen at work too?

Yes. The same pattern shows up when colleagues avoid tasks by doing them poorly, and the same fix applies: clear ownership of outcomes.

Should I call it weaponised incompetence to my partner?

It is usually better not to start with the label. Describe the specific pattern instead, which keeps the conversation about the arrangement rather than about character.

What if my partner really cannot do the task?

Then hand over a different area that suits them better. The aim is a fair split overall, not that both partners can do everything.
