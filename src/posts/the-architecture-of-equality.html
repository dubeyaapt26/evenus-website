To rebalance the workload in a relationship, work through five steps: list every household task, including the planning; estimate how long each one takes each week; compare how much free time each of you has left; move one whole area of responsibility from the busier partner to the other; then review after a month. The order matters. Couples who jump straight to swapping chores usually end up arguing about whose list is right.

This is the practical version. If you first want to settle what "fair" should mean in your house, read what makes a fair marriage at home. If the conversation itself is the hard part, how to talk to your partner about splitting chores covers what to say.

Throughout, we will follow Jess and Dev. Both work full-time. They have a five-year-old. Jess feels permanently busy. Dev feels he does a lot and is not sure what has gone wrong.

Step 1: List everything, separately

Each of you writes down every recurring household task you do, on your own, without comparing. Give it a week if you can, adding things as they happen.

Include the obvious physical work: cooking, cleaning, laundry, bins, school runs. Then add the work that leaves no trace: booking the dentist, replying to the nursery, noticing the washing powder is low, remembering the MOT, planning meals, buying birthday presents, checking the bank balance before a big shop. The invisible labour checklist is a useful prompt if you get stuck.

Why separately? Because the differences between your lists are information. When Jess and Dev compare, Dev has 14 items and Jess has 38. Dev is not hiding anything. He genuinely did not know that "school uniform" was a job that needed noticing, sizing, ordering and labelling.

Step 2: Estimate the time, and mark the planning

Put a rough weekly time against each task and mark the ones that involve planning, remembering or noticing. Rough is fine. You are looking for a shape, not a timesheet.

The physical hours look less lopsided than Jess feels: on this sample, 12 hours for Jess against 7.5 for Dev. What the table also shows is that most of the planning sits on Jess's side. Planning takes more time than any single task on the list suggests, because it keeps running in the background. That is the mental load, and it is why the hours alone undercount Jess's week.

Step 3: Compare free time, not chore lists

Work out how many hours each of you has left after sleep, paid work, commuting and household work. This is the number that tells you whether the workload is out of balance.

Jess and Dev both sleep about 56 hours a week. Jess works 37.5 hours plus 5 hours of commuting. Dev works 40 plus 7.5. With all their household work counted, Jess does 24 hours and Dev does 13.

  • Jess: 168 − 56 − 37.5 − 5 − 24 = 45.5 hours
  • Dev: 168 − 56 − 40 − 7.5 − 13 = 51.5 hours

Six hours a week. Not dramatic, but it is an evening and a half, every week, and it adds up to the tiredness Jess describes. Dev's longer commute and hours are counted. The gap is still there. You can try your own numbers in the fairness calculator.

Step 4: Move one whole area, not a handful of tasks

Pick one area of the house and transfer all of it, including the noticing and deciding, to the partner with more free time. One complete transfer will close more of the gap than five small favours.

Jess and Dev look at the table. School admin is 2 hours of clock time but carries a lot of background planning. Dev takes it: he becomes the named contact, reads the emails, adds the dates to the shared calendar and handles the forms. Jess does not check it.

Good areas to move are ones that are self-contained and that come with a clear outcome. Some examples:

  • All meals on weekdays, from planning to clearing up
  • Everything to do with the car
  • Children's clothes and shoes
  • Birthdays and presents for one side of the family
  • Household bills and renewals

Agree out loud that the new owner will do it their way. If it comes back for approval, it has not moved. The difference between helping out and taking ownership goes deeper into why this matters.

Step 5: Review after a month, then adjust

Give the change four weeks, then look at the numbers again and decide whether to make another move. One month is long enough for the new habit to settle and short enough that a mistake does not harden.

At the review, ask three questions:

  1. Did the transferred area stay transferred, or did it drift back?
  2. Has the free-time gap narrowed?
  3. Is either of you now overloaded? If both of you are stretched, the fix is less work overall, not a better split.

Jess and Dev find school admin has stuck. The gap is down to about three hours. They agree to look again in a month, and to move weekday dinners on two nights if it has not closed further.

What gets in the way of rebalancing?

The usual obstacles are different standards, a habit of asking instead of owning, and changes in life that nobody adjusted for. Each has a simple first move.

  • Different standards: agree which standards are real (the bathroom gets cleaned weekly) and which are preference (how towels are folded).
  • Asking instead of owning: if the new owner keeps checking in, agree they decide and only report problems.
  • Life changes: a new baby, a promotion or a job loss resets everything. Re-run Step 3.

Common questions

How long does it take to rebalance chores?

The first move can happen this week. Closing a sizeable gap usually takes two or three transfers over a few months, with a review after each.

What if we disagree on how long tasks take?

Use the lower estimate for now and look at the free-time totals. If the gap is still there with the conservative numbers, the disagreement does not change the conclusion.

Should we use a chore chart?

A chart records who does what, but someone still has to maintain it. Owning whole areas tends to last longer than ticking off a list.

What if my partner has more free time but much less energy?

Capacity matters as much as hours. Illness, a demanding job or a hard season can justify a different split, as long as you both agree it and revisit it.
