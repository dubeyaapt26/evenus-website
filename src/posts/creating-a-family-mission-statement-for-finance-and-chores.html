A family mission statement for money and chores is a short written agreement, about half a page, that names what you both care about and turns it into rules you can follow on an ordinary Tuesday. It should include two or three shared values, how you split bills, how much each of you can spend freely, who owns which parts of the housework, and when you will review it. If it does not change a single decision you make next week, it is a poster, not a mission statement.

Most templates online stop at the values. That is the easy part. The useful part is the translation from "we value fairness" into "each of us keeps £250 a month, no questions asked".

What is a family mission statement for money and chores?

It is a household's operating agreement: the reasons behind your choices about money and housework, written next to the choices themselves. Companies write mission statements to explain what they are for. A household version does the same job at a smaller scale, and it works best when it is specific.

It earns its place in three situations. When you are setting up a home together and have never discussed how money will work. When a change, such as a baby, a move or a job loss, has broken the old arrangement. And when the same argument keeps coming back, which usually means you are disagreeing about values while talking about the bins.

How do you write one, step by step?

Do it in two short sessions rather than one long one, and write separately before you write together. Writing separately first stops the more confident speaker setting the agenda.

Step 1: Answer five questions on your own

  1. What did money feel like in the house you grew up in?
  2. What do you want money to do for us in the next five years?
  3. What does a fair split of housework look like to you, in practice?
  4. What is one thing about how we run the house now that you would keep?
  5. What is one thing you would change?

Fifteen minutes each, written down, then swap and read before talking.

Step 2: Find two or three shared values

Look for overlap rather than trying to win. Common ones are security, freedom, fairness, generosity, time together and simplicity. Three is the limit. A list of eight values cannot guide a decision because something on it will always justify anything.

Step 3: Turn each value into a rule

This is the step most couples skip, and it is the one that matters. For each value, ask: "What would we do differently this month if we took this seriously?"

ValueRule it becomes
SecurityWe keep three months of shared bills in an easy-access savings account before saving for anything else
FreedomEach of us has a personal amount every month that the other never asks about
FairnessBills are split in proportion to income, and we each end the week with a similar amount of time that is our own
SimplicityEvery repeating bill leaves by direct debit from one account
Time togetherOne evening a week has no household admin in it

Step 4: Write it on half a page

One paragraph of values, then the rules as a short list. Date it. Keep it somewhere you will both see it at least once a month.

Step 5: Set a review date

Every six months, and after any big change. A mission statement that is never revisited turns into a rule book one person enforces.

What does a good one look like?

Specific enough to settle a disagreement, short enough to remember. Here is one written by Sam and Alex, who have been living together for two years. Sam takes home £2,600 a month and Alex £1,900.

We want our home to feel calm, and we want both of us to have a life outside it. We care about security, fairness and time together.

Money. Shared bills are split in proportion to income. Each of us keeps £200 a month that is ours alone. Anything over £150 for the house, we mention first. We keep £6,000 in shared savings before we save for the car.

Chores. Sam owns food and money admin. Alex owns laundry, cleaning and the car. Whoever owns an area also does the remembering for it. We do it differently and that is allowed.

Review. First Sunday of April and October, or after any big change.

The arithmetic behind their split: together they take home £4,500. Sam's share is 2,600 ÷ 4,500, about 58%, and Alex's about 42%. On £2,000 of shared bills, Sam pays about £1,156 and Alex about £844. They leave a small buffer in the joint account for the pennies. How to split bills as a couple walks through setting this up month by month.

What should go in the chores section?

Areas and owners, not a task list. A list of forty tasks goes out of date in a fortnight. A short list of who owns which area of the home stays true.

  • Name four to six areas: food, laundry, cleaning, money and admin, children, home and repairs, social life.
  • Give each one a single owner.
  • Say explicitly that owning an area includes noticing when it needs doing.
  • Agree which standards are real (clothes are clean) and which are preference (how they are folded).

That third point is what most agreements miss. The planning and remembering part of housework, sometimes called cognitive labour, is easy to leave out of a written agreement because nobody sees it being done. Dividing housework without it becoming an argument explains why ownership of whole areas works better than splitting a list.

What if your values clash?

Write down both, and make a rule that lets each of you act on yours within a limit. Clashes are normal, and most are about security against freedom: one of you wants to save more, the other wants to enjoy the money now.

The personal allowance is the tool for this. It lets the saver see the savings rule kept, and it lets the spender spend without asking. A rule like "we save £400 a month together, and each of us spends our own £200 however we like" honours both values at once.

If you find you cannot agree on the basics, such as whether to share information about debts or income, that is worth talking through before you write anything down. How to talk about money without fighting covers how to have that conversation, and financial transparency covers what genuinely needs to be shared.

How do you make it stick?

Tie it to something that already happens. A mission statement survives when it is read at a moment you already have, not one you have to invent.

  • Read the rules section at your monthly settle-up.
  • Open the chores section when something changes at work or at home.
  • Use the review date to ask one question: which rule did we actually follow, and which did we ignore?

The rules you ignored are the useful ones to discuss. Either the rule was wrong, or something in your week makes it impossible, and both are worth knowing. EvenUS can help with the second, because it shows how time and money are actually being shared week by week, which makes the review a look at what happened rather than two memories of it.

Common questions

Should children be involved in a family mission statement?

Older children can help with the values and with their own jobs around the house. Keep the money rules between the adults, and share the parts that affect the children in plain terms.

How long should a family mission statement be?

Half a page is enough. If it runs longer, it is probably trying to cover every situation, and it will be harder to remember when you need it.

Is a family mission statement the same as a budget?

No. A budget says where the money goes this month. The mission statement says why, and sets the rules the budget follows. Budgeting for couples covers the budget itself.

Do we need to sign it?

No. It is not a legal document, and treating it as one tends to make people defensive. Dating it is enough, so you know which version you agreed.
