When one partner has a chronic illness, run the home by capacity rather than equal shares. Sort tasks by how much energy they take and how flexible their timing is, give the unwell partner work that fits their good days, keep two plans for steady weeks and flare weeks, cut or outsource what you can, and protect the well partner from quietly burning out.

None of this is medical advice. How a condition affects someone's energy, concentration and mobility is a question for their doctor or an occupational therapist. This is about the household side: how two people keep a home running fairly when one of them has less to give, and how much less changes from week to week.

What does fair look like when one partner is chronically ill?

Fair means both partners end up with a similar amount of rest and life that is genuinely their own, not that both do the same number of chores. Equal tasks would be unfair to the person who is unwell. Doing everything for them would be unfair to the other person, and often takes away a sense of contributing that matters to the person who is ill.

The healthier question is not "who does more?" but "what can each of us realistically carry this month, and does the arrangement leave both of us with something left?" Why fair does not always mean equal explains the general principle.

How do you divide chores when energy changes day to day?

Sort tasks by energy cost and by timing, then match them to each person's capacity. Many conditions come with unpredictable energy, so a task that must happen at 7pm on a Tuesday is a poor fit even if it is light.

That last row needs a warning. It is tempting to give all the planning to the person who cannot do the physical work, on the grounds that "at least they can do that from the sofa." Many chronic conditions bring fatigue that affects thinking as well as the body. Planning and remembering are tiring too. The mental load is real work, and piling all of it onto one partner swaps one imbalance for another.

A worked example: Ravi and Meera

Ravi has a condition that flares several times a year. Meera works full-time. Between flares, Ravi works part-time from home and has moderate energy. During a flare, he can do very little for one to three weeks.

They keep two plans.

Steady weeks

  • Ravi owns: the online food order, bills and household admin, laundry (seated folding), weekday dinners using a simple rotation.
  • Meera owns: cleaning, the bins and recycling, weekend cooking, anything involving lifting or driving.
  • Shared: family birthdays and visits, alternating months.

Flare weeks

  • Ravi keeps: only what he chooses, usually the online order if he can manage it.
  • Meera takes: the essentials only. Clean clothes, food, bins, medicines collected.
  • Dropped: deep cleaning, non-urgent admin, social plans.
  • Bought in: ready meals, and a cleaner fortnightly if the flare runs past a week.

The flare plan is agreed in a steady week, when both of them can think clearly. That avoids negotiating on the worst day of the month.

How do you stop the well partner burning out?

Treat the carer's load as real work, cut the household down to essentials when needed, and make sure they keep some time that is theirs. The well partner often becomes nurse, driver, admin department and earner, and the role grows so gradually that nobody notices until they are exhausted.

Signs to watch for: the well partner has stopped seeing friends, is the only one who knows how anything works, or feels guilty taking an evening off. A few things help:

  • Write down what the caring involves, including medical admin such as appointments, prescriptions and forms. Making it visible makes it discussable.
  • Lower standards on purpose. Dust is optional. Different standards of mess covers how to agree what actually matters.
  • Bring in outside help where you can: family, friends, paid help or local carers' organisations.
  • Protect at least one regular block of time each week for the well partner to rest or see people.

Caring for ageing parents and the cost of being the household manager cover similar ground for other caring situations.

Who should manage medical admin?

Where possible, the person who is ill, with the other as backup. Owning their own appointments and medication keeps autonomy where it belongs, and many people prefer it. In flare periods, the partner steps in, using a shared record of medicines, contacts and upcoming appointments so nothing depends on one person's memory.

How do you handle money when illness affects income?

Change your split method so both of you keep some money of your own. If one partner works less or stops working, an equal split no longer makes sense. A proportional split, or an equal remainder split that protects a personal allowance for each of you first, usually fits better. How to split bills when you earn different amounts runs through each method with worked numbers.

Questions about disability benefits, insurance claims, sick pay or long-term financial planning need a qualified adviser or an advice charity that specialises in your situation.

Can an app help?

An app cannot treat anything, and it should not pretend to. What EvenUS can do is show how the household is actually shared, with each partner logging privately. One caveat: any measure based on hours counts a day in bed during a flare as hours not spent on chores. It cannot see that those hours were not rest in any real sense. Read the numbers with that in mind. EvenUS flags weeks where both partners are overloaded rather than calling them balanced, and if things stay difficult for a long stretch it stops suggesting swaps and points towards a counsellor.

For the illness itself, the right people are your doctor, specialist team or occupational therapist. For the strain on the relationship, a counsellor experienced with illness in couples can help both of you.

Common questions

Is it fair for the healthy partner to do most of the housework?

Often, yes, if that leaves both of you with a similar amount of rest and personal time. Fairness is about what each person has left, not about equal tasks.

How do you talk about chores without making the ill partner feel guilty?

Talk about the arrangement, not the person. "The flare plan is not covering laundry" is easier to hear than a list of what someone did not do.

What if we disagree about how much the unwell partner can do?

That is common, and a doctor or occupational therapist can give you both a clearer picture. Start with what the unwell partner says they can manage, and review together after a few weeks.

Should we hire help?

If you can afford it, buying in cleaning, meals or gardening during difficult periods is one of the most effective ways to protect both partners.
