When the last child leaves home, the housework shrinks but the roles you built around raising them usually stay exactly where they were. The fix is to audit what actually disappeared, what stayed, and what is new, then re-divide the household on purpose: whole areas of ownership, the mental load of adult children included, and a fresh look at how you split money. This applies whether you are 45 or 60, and whether you both still work.

Retirement is a different shift, because paid work stops. That has its own guide on sharing the household in retirement. This one is about the quieter change that often comes first: the house empties, and the arrangement you designed for a family of four is suddenly running a home for two.

What actually changes when the kids leave home?

Less than you expect, and unevenly. School runs, packed lunches and most of the laundry go. The planning of family life does not vanish. It moves onto the phone.

Sort your household tasks into four groups. The pattern is usually something like this:

GroupTypical examples
DisappearsSchool runs, parents' evenings, uniform shopping, driving to clubs, homework supervision
ShrinksCooking, food shopping, laundry, tidying, the general noise of a busy house
Stays the sameBills, car, garden, repairs, insurance renewals, cleaning the bathroom
New or growingKeeping in touch with the children, university or rent admin, sending parcels, planning visits home, often caring for ageing parents

Notice what sits in each row. The tasks that disappear are mostly the ones built around children, and in many households one person carried most of them. The tasks that stay are often the other person's. So one partner may gain a lot of time and the other almost none, and neither of you planned that.

Why do the old roles stick around?

Because roles are habits with an identity attached, and nobody formally ends them. For eighteen or twenty years one of you was probably the family manager: the one who knew the dentist's number, the permission slip deadline and which child had fallen out with whom. That job did not get a leaving date.

Arlie Hochschild's book The Second Shift (1989) described the unpaid housework that begins after paid work ends. Parenting years tend to lock that second shift into a fixed pattern. When the children go, the pattern keeps running on momentum. The family manager keeps managing a family that is now mostly elsewhere, and the other partner keeps doing their established jobs, because nobody suggested anything else.

It is worth saying plainly that this is the system, not either person. Both partners are doing what the old arrangement asked of them.

A worked example: Priya and Tom

Priya is 52 and Tom is 54. Both work full-time. Their youngest, Anika, has just moved to Leeds for university.

Before Anika left, Priya spent about 24 hours a week on household work, including most of the cooking for four, school logistics and the family calendar. Tom spent about 12, mainly the car, garden, bills and DIY.

Three months later they each estimate their week again:

  • Priya: cooking and shopping fall from 9 hours to 5. School and club logistics fall from 5 hours to 0. Laundry and tidying fall from 6 to 3. But calls and messages with Anika, her student finance forms, and posting a forgotten coat add 4 hours. Total: 24 down to 16.
  • Tom: garden, car, bills and repairs stay at 11. Lifts to the station and helping with homework disappear, saving 1. Total: 12 down to 11.

Priya gained 8 hours and Tom gained 1. That looks like good news for Priya, and in hours it is. The gap is still 16 against 11, though, and most of Priya's remaining hours are the planning-heavy kind: remembering, noticing and organising. If you weight the mental load properly, the gap is wider than the raw numbers suggest.

The bigger point: this is the one moment in a long relationship when you can rebalance without taking anything away from anyone. There is spare capacity in the system. Use it deliberately, or it will be absorbed by whatever is nearest.

How do empty nesters re-divide the chores?

Treat it like setting up a new household, because in practical terms you are. Five steps, over one or two evenings.

  1. List what is left. Write down every recurring task for a two-person home. It will be shorter than you think.
  2. Formally retire the old jobs. Say out loud that the school calendar, the kids' dentist and the packed-lunch shop are gone. It sounds trivial. It releases the person who was still half-holding them.
  3. Hand over whole areas, not tasks. "Dinners on weekdays are yours" moves the planning as well as the cooking. "Can you cook tonight?" does not. The difference is explained in helping out versus taking ownership.
  4. Assign the new work explicitly. Contact with adult children, their admin and visits home need an owner, or they default to the old family manager.
  5. Set a review date. Three months on, check whether it still feels right. The first term away is rarely how things settle.

For the conversation itself, how to divide housework without it becoming an argument covers why owning areas works better than splitting a list.

Who handles the adult children now?

Share it, and name it as work. Adult children generate a different kind of load: the late-night call about a landlord, the bank transfer for a broken laptop, the holiday logistics when they come home with laundry.

Practical ways to split it:

  • Each partner owns a channel. One handles money questions, the other handles housing and admin.
  • Each partner owns a child, if you have more than one, with a weekly swap of news so nobody is the only one who knows what is going on.
  • Visits home get one planner per visit, alternating.

Children often call the parent they always called. That is fine. The other partner can still own the follow-up, so the one who answers the phone is not also the one who books, pays and remembers.

What about money when the nest empties?

Revisit the split, because the household budget has changed shape. Food and child costs drop, university support may rise, and incomes may have moved a lot since you last chose a method.

Suppose Priya takes home £3,000 a month and Tom £3,500. Shared costs used to be £3,400. With Anika gone they are £2,600, plus £400 a month towards her rent. Is that £400 a shared cost or not? Most couples treat it as shared. Split in proportion to income, they pay 46% and 54% of £3,000: about £1,385 and £1,615. The method matters less than choosing it again rather than inheriting it. What if it is about more than chores?

For some people the quiet house is a loss, not a relief, especially the partner whose days were built around the children. Re-dividing the housework will not fix that, and it is not meant to. If low mood or a sense of purposelessness lasts for weeks, talking to a GP or a counsellor is a sensible step, and couples counselling can help if the change has opened up distance between you.

Common questions

How long does it take to adjust to an empty nest?

There is no set time. Many couples find the first few months unsettled, then a new routine forms. Reviewing your household arrangement at around three months gives you a real picture rather than a first-term one.

Should we downsize when the kids leave?

That is a financial and personal decision best made with proper advice. From a household point of view, a smaller home usually means less upkeep, but moving is a large project in itself, so agree who owns which parts of it.

What if one of us wants to keep doing everything the old way?

Start with one area rather than a full renegotiation. Ask the person holding most of the old role which single job they would most like to stop thinking about, and transfer that one completely.

What happens when an adult child moves back home?

Treat it as another household change and list the tasks again. An adult living at home can own areas too, and agreeing that at the start avoids the old roles quietly returning.
