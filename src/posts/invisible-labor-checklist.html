Invisible labour is the household work nobody sees being done: noticing, planning, remembering, booking and checking. This checklist lists 25 hidden household tasks in five groups, from restocking and meal planning to money admin, family logistics and keeping in touch. Go through it separately, mark who usually does each one, then compare. The items that only one of you ticked are usually where the imbalance sits.

Most of these tasks take minutes. What makes them heavy is that they never finish and nobody notices them until they are missed. If you want the background first, what the mental load is explains the idea in plain terms.

How to use this checklist

Each of you goes through it alone, marks each task, then you compare. Doing it side by side tends to produce polite answers rather than honest ones.

For each task, write one of these:

  • Me: I almost always do it, including remembering it needs doing.
  • Mostly me: I usually do it, sometimes with prompting the other way.
  • Shared: genuinely split, and neither of us has to remind the other.
  • Mostly you or You: the reverse.
  • Nobody: it does not happen, or doesn't apply.

The rule that matters: count a task as yours only if you also notice when it needs doing. Doing it when asked is helping with someone else's task.

Food and household supplies

The restocking work: knowing what the house needs before it runs out.

  • 1. Noticing what's running low. Milk, loo roll, dishwasher tablets, the children's toothpaste.
  • 2. Planning the week's meals. Deciding what everyone eats, around who is home when and who will not eat what.
  • 3. Writing the shopping list. Turning the meal plan and the running-low list into something someone else could shop from.
  • 4. Using things up before they go off. Knowing what is in the fridge and what needs eating first.
  • 5. Replacing worn-out household items. Towels, pans, the broken hoover, the bedding that is past it.

Money and household admin

The paperwork that only gets noticed when it goes wrong.

  • 6. Checking bills and bank statements. Spotting the price rise or the payment that failed.
  • 7. Tracking renewal dates. Car insurance, home insurance, broadband, phone contracts.
  • 8. Comparing providers and switching. The hour of research that saves money nobody thanks you for.
  • 9. Managing subscriptions. Remembering what you pay for and cancelling what you no longer use.
  • 10. Sorting post and important documents. Knowing where the passports, the boiler warranty and the tenancy agreement are.

Health, home and pets

The booking, chasing and remembering that keeps everyone and everything running.

  • 11. Booking medical and dental appointments. For yourself, the children, and sometimes your partner.
  • 12. Keeping track of prescriptions. Noticing when they are due and arranging collection.
  • 13. Arranging repairs and tradespeople. Getting quotes, booking a slot, being in, and following up when they do not turn up.
  • 14. Remembering servicing and checks. The boiler, the car's MOT and service, the smoke alarm batteries.
  • 15. Pet admin. Vaccinations, flea treatment, food orders, finding someone to look after them when you are away.

Children and family logistics

The coordination underneath every school day and family weekend. Skip this group if it does not apply.

  • 16. Tracking school dates and forms. Inset days, trip consent, dress-up days, payments.
  • 17. Knowing when clothes and shoes no longer fit. And replacing them before the morning they are needed.
  • 18. Arranging childcare cover. Holidays, sick days and the evening you both have a work event.
  • 19. Organising activities, playdates and parties. Signing up, paying, buying the present, replying to the invitation.
  • 20. Packing for trips and days out. Knowing what each person needs, and remembering the charger.

Social life and keeping in touch

The kin work: keeping relationships with family and friends going.

  • 21. Remembering birthdays and buying presents. Including your partner's side of the family.
  • 22. Planning the social calendar. Making sure weekends do not clash and that you do actually see friends.
  • 23. Checking in on ageing parents and relatives. Calls, visits, noticing when something seems wrong.
  • 24. Sending cards, thank-yous and condolences. The small gestures other people remember.
  • 25. Planning holidays and occasions. Research, booking, and the dozen decisions in between.

Some of this last group overlaps with emotional labour: managing how people feel, not just what happens. The two are related but different, as emotional labour versus mental load explains.

What do your answers mean?

Look at three things: the count, the gaps, and the "nobody" column.

Maya and Jordan did this separately. Maya marked 17 of the 25 as "me" or "mostly me". Jordan marked 11 of those 17 as "shared". Neither was lying. Jordan did help with most of them, and Maya was the one noticing they needed doing. That gap, the difference between helping and owning, was the real finding.

  • If one person has most of the ticks, the planning is concentrated in one place. That is a setup to change, not a fault.
  • If you disagree about the same task, you are seeing different parts of it. Ask, "What part of this do you not see me doing?"
  • If several tasks are "nobody", decide whether they matter. Some can genuinely be dropped.

A long list of "me" can feel heavy to look at. If it does, seven signs you're carrying the mental load may put words to it, and if the tiredness has gone on for months, talk to a GP or counsellor.

What should you do with the list?

Choose one group, not one task, and move it. Handing over "renewal dates" alone leaves the other money admin with the same person. Handing over the whole money and admin group moves the noticing as well.

  1. Pick the group with the biggest gap.
  2. Agree who takes it for the next month, including the remembering.
  3. Hand over what the new owner needs: logins, dates, where the documents live.
  4. Let them do it their way. A different method is not a wrong one.
  5. Check in after a month.

If booking and admin are the heaviest part for you, the one who books everything goes into that group in detail. For a longer list that also includes physical chores, see the ultimate mental load checklist.

Common questions

Is invisible labour the same as the mental load?

They overlap heavily. Invisible labour is the broader term for any unnoticed household work. The mental load is the planning and remembering part of it.

Why do I feel tired when the tasks are so small?

Because they never finish. Holding twenty-five open loops in your head is tiring even when each one takes five minutes to act on.

Should we split these 25 tasks evenly?

Not necessarily task by task. Aim for each of you owning whole groups, so the remembering moves along with the doing.

Can I print this checklist?

Yes. Print two copies so you can each fill one in privately before comparing.
