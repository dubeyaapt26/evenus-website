"Who did more?" fights end when couples stop comparing lists of tasks and start comparing something both can agree on: how much time each person had left that was genuinely their own, including the planning nobody sees. Then you change one specific thing, try it for a fortnight, and review it calmly. Counting harder never settles it, because you are each counting different things.

This piece explains why the fight keeps coming back, and a way out that does not need anyone to win.

Why does the "who did more?" argument keep happening?

Because each of you sees your own work in full and your partner's only in pieces. Both of you can be sincere and both can feel you do more.

Take Sam and Alex on a Tuesday evening. Alex has just emptied the dishwasher, put a wash on and done bath time. Sam has spent the evening on the sofa with a laptop, which looks like rest. It is actually the household's admin: rebooking the boiler service, answering the nursery's email about Friday, and working out whether the car insurance renewal is worth shopping around.

Alex says, "I've done everything tonight." Sam says, "You have no idea what I've done tonight." Both are right about their own evening and partly blind to the other's. A list on the fridge would show Alex winning comfortably, because the fridge list only records things you can see being done.

The physical work is visible. The planning, remembering and chasing is not. Allison Daminger's 2019 research on cognitive household labour breaks that hidden part into anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding and monitoring. Almost none of it appears on a chore chart. What the mental load is covers it in more detail.

Why doesn't keeping score fix it?

Because a scoreboard turns two people sharing a home into opponents, and whoever is losing on the visible count will dispute the count.

Couples often try a points chart or a tally. It works for a week. Then the arguments move from "who does more" to "that should be worth more points", "you didn't log that", and "doing it badly doesn't count". The score becomes another thing to fight about. How keeping score damages relationships covers that trap.

Scoreboards also punish whoever had a hard week. A sick child, a deadline or a bad month of sleep shows up as "losing", which is the opposite of what a partner needs to hear at that moment.

What do you compare instead?

Compare free time, not tasks. Everyone has 168 hours a week. Take out sleep, paid work, commuting and household work, and see what each of you has left.

For Sam and Alex, a rough week looks like this:

Hours after sleep and personal care9898
Paid work and commute4538
Household work, including planning2127
Time genuinely their own3233

Sam: 98 minus 45 minus 21 is 32. Alex: 98 minus 38 minus 27 is 33. They are almost even. The fight was never about one of them coasting. Alex does more hands-on work and Sam does more paid work plus the admin, and neither could see the other's share.

That is a different conversation. Not "you do nothing", but "I didn't know the nursery emails were taking your evenings". You can run the same sum with your own numbers in the household fairness calculator.

How do you stop the fight in practice?

Five steps, done on a calm evening rather than in the middle of a row.

  1. Each of you lists your week separately, including the planning: booking, remembering, chasing, deciding. Estimates are fine.
  2. Swap lists and ask one question: "What on here did I not know you were doing?" Do not argue with the other list.
  3. Compare hours left, not task counts. If the gap is small, the problem is visibility. If it is large, the problem is distribution.
  4. Pick one thing to move, with a reason that is about timing, not blame. "Your Saturday mornings are lighter, so the food order fits there."
  5. Try it for two weeks, then review. Keep it or hand it back. Both are fine answers.

The language matters as much as the steps. Name the system, not the person: "most of the planning sits with one of us right now" lands very differently from "you never plan anything". Dividing labour without blame or guilt has more wording that helps.

How does EvenUS approach the "who did more?" fight?

It measures time, money and mental load without turning them into a scoreboard, shows where you see things differently, and suggests one swap a week.

No winner, no loser

There are no streaks, badges, points or leaderboards, and the score is never shared publicly. Neither of you can edit or dispute the other's entries, because a dispute button is a fight generator. There is no way to nudge your partner and no notification about what they did or did not do.

Disagreements shown, not judged

When Sam says money admin is mostly his and Alex says it is shared, the app shows both answers with equal weight under "seen differently". It does not rule on who is right. That gap is often the real finding, and it is far more useful than a tally.

One change, with a reason

Each week the app suggests one specific swap: who, what, and why now. Either of you can say no, and no note goes to the other about it. If you both agree, it runs for two weeks. How to read your weekly fairness report explains how to use it.

What it does not claim

EvenUS does not fix conflict or save relationships. It helps you find out what the argument is actually about, and do one thing about it this week. If things stay stuck for about two months, it stops suggesting swaps, says so once, points toward a counsellor, and goes quiet.

When is the fight about something else?

When the same argument survives a fair split, it is usually about feeling unseen, unappreciated or alone, not about the dishes.

Saying thank you for specific, invisible things helps more than people expect: "Thanks for sorting the nursery thing, I know it ate your evening." If the arguments are frequent, frightening or tied to bigger issues such as money worries or resentment that has built up over years, a qualified couples counsellor is the right next step.

Common questions

Is it normal to argue about chores?

Yes, very. Housework is a frequent source of everyday friction between partners, partly because so much of it is invisible to the person not doing it.

Does "fair" mean 50/50?

No. Fair usually means both of you end up with a similar amount of time that is genuinely your own, which may involve one person doing more at home while the other does more paid work.

What if my partner won't track anything?

Start with the conversation, not the tool. Ask for one week of rough estimates as an experiment, and frame it as finding out, not proving a point.

How do I stop keeping score in my head?

Replace the tally with a weekly review. Once you know there is a calm time to raise it, the urge to count in the moment usually fades.
