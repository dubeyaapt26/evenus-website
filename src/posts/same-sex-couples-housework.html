Research on household labour has found consistently that same-sex couples divide domestic work more evenly than different-sex couples do. The most useful finding is not that they are better at it, but why the difference exists: there is no default script telling either person which jobs are theirs, so the division has to be negotiated out loud.

That is worth understanding whoever you live with, because the mechanism is available to any household. It just costs a conversation that most couples never have.

What the research actually says

Two findings recur across studies of same-sex households, and it is worth being precise about both.

The split is more even, not perfectly even. Same-sex couples report more equal divisions of housework and childcare, but not a fifty-fifty one. Specialisation still happens. Someone still becomes the person who cooks.

Where it specialises, it does so along different lines. In different-sex couples the split correlates with gender in fairly predictable ways. In same-sex couples it correlates more with things like working hours, preference and who is better at a given task. The specialisation is chosen rather than assumed.

The caveat worth keeping: much of this work relies on self-report, sample sizes are usually smaller than for different-sex couples, and "more even" is a comparison rather than a description. Plenty of same-sex households have exactly the imbalance this site is about.

Why does the absence of a script matter so much?

Because most household divisions are never decided. They are inherited, and then defended after the fact.

When both people arrive with a default about who handles the car and who handles the birthday cards, the arrangement forms without anyone choosing it. Nobody has to say anything, which feels efficient, and it means the arrangement is never examined. Years later, questioning it feels like an accusation, because by then it looks like a decision somebody made.

Without a script, that shortcut is not available. Two people who both grew up expecting to do the cooking have to work out which of them is doing it, and they have to do that early and explicitly. The negotiation is not more virtuous. It is simply mandatory.

The lesson is available to anyone: the arrangements that hold up are the ones that were said out loud. That is the whole of it, and it has nothing to do with who you live with.

Does that mean the mental load is not an issue?

No, and this is where an evenly-split house can still go wrong.

The physical tasks are what get negotiated, because they are what people can see. The mental load, the noticing and remembering and deciding, still tends to settle on whoever is more inclined to notice. That inclination is not distributed by gender, so it distributes by temperament instead, and one person still ends up holding the household's running order in their head.

A couple can genuinely split the chores down the middle and still have one person doing all the anticipating. That is the imbalance the tasks are hiding. What the mental load is covers what to look for.

What tends to differ, and what does not

Some things do work differently in same-sex households, and glossing over that is its own kind of unhelpful.

Income gaps are just as common. The idea that same-sex couples earn similar amounts is not supported. The same splitting questions apply, and so does the same warning about defaulting to fifty-fifty when incomes differ. Splitting bills when you earn different amounts runs the arithmetic.

Legal and financial structures vary by country in ways that affect who owns what and who is liable for what. That is worth actual advice from someone qualified in your jurisdiction rather than a blog post.

Family-of-origin load can be uneven in a specific way. If one partner's family is engaged and the other's is not, the maintenance of family relationships, which is real work, sits disproportionately with one person. It rarely appears on any list.

Parenting divisions still form. In couples with children, one partner often does more of the daily caregiving, and the pattern tracks working hours and preference rather than anything else. The point is that it was arrived at rather than assumed.

What every household can take from this

Three things, and none requires anyone to change who they are.

Decide it rather than inherit it. Say who has what, out loud, once. An arrangement that was chosen can be revisited without it being an accusation, which is the property that matters most in five years' time.

Assign by capacity and preference, not by symmetry. The measure worth equalising is what each person has left at the end of the week, not the number of tasks each does. Fair does not always mean equal covers why.

Divide the noticing, not just the doing. This is the part that survives least well without deliberate effort in any household. Give someone a whole area including the remembering, rather than a list of tasks within it. Dividing housework without an argument goes into how.

A note on how this site is written

Most writing about household labour, including a good deal of the earlier writing here, describes a man and a woman and takes that as the general case. It is not the general case, and where the research is about different-sex couples specifically, this site should say so rather than leave it implied.

The measurement itself has never cared. EvenUS compares two people in a household and has no concept of who they are to each other. There is no default about who does what, which is the point.
