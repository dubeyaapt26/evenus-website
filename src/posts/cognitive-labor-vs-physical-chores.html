Physical chores are the hands-on part of housework: cooking, cleaning, washing, driving. Cognitive labour is the thinking behind them: anticipating what is needed, researching options, deciding, and checking it happened. The difference matters because a couple can split physical chores evenly while one partner does almost all the cognitive work, and a chore list will make that look fair when it is not. To see the real division, you need to count both.

What is cognitive labour?

Cognitive labour is the mental work of managing a household. The clearest definition comes from the sociologist Allison Daminger, whose 2019 paper "The Cognitive Dimensions of Household Labor" in the American Sociological Review broke it into four parts:

  1. Anticipating needs. Seeing in advance that something will need doing. The children's winter coats will not fit this year.
  2. Identifying options. Finding the possible ways to meet that need. Three shops, a second-hand site, a cousin with older children.
  3. Deciding. Choosing between the options, usually with a budget in mind.
  4. Monitoring. Following up to make sure the need was met and the result worked. Do the coats fit? Did the refund come through?

Daminger found that in the couples she studied, anticipating and monitoring were especially likely to be held by one partner, while deciding was more often shared. That is a useful detail. A couple can make decisions together and still have one person doing all the noticing that leads up to them and all the checking that follows.

"Cognitive labour" and "the mental load" are often used to mean the same thing. If you want the everyday version of the idea, what the mental load is explains it without the academic framing.

What are physical chores?

Physical chores are the tasks you can watch someone do. Washing up, vacuuming, laundry, cooking, the school run, mowing the lawn, taking the car for its service. They take a fairly predictable amount of time, they have a clear finish, and they leave visible evidence.

That visibility is why most chore charts, rotas and apps track only physical chores. They are easy to list and easy to tick off. The thinking that sets them up is not.

A worked example: one dentist appointment

Breaking one task into its parts shows how much of it is thinking. Here is a routine dental check-up for a child, with rough time estimates.

StepTypeTime
Remembering it has been six monthsCognitive (anticipating)2 min
Checking the calendar for a free afternoon around schoolCognitive (options)5 min
Phoning the practice and bookingCognitive (deciding)10 min
Arranging time off work, telling the schoolCognitive (deciding)5 min

Add it up: 29 minutes of cognitive work and 75 minutes of physical work. On a chore list this appears as "dentist, 75 minutes". Now suppose Jordan does the driving and Maya does everything else. The list says Jordan took the child to the dentist. In fact Maya did 29 minutes of work across three weeks, in five separate pieces, any of which could have been forgotten.

Most of the minutes were physical. Most of the responsibility was cognitive. That pattern repeats across almost every household task.

Why do both need to be tracked?

Because tracking only physical chores hides the imbalance it is meant to reveal. When couples write down who does what, they usually list physical tasks, find the split looks reasonable, and leave the conversation feeling that one person was overstating it. The work that was missing from the list is exactly the work in dispute.

Tracking only cognitive labour does not work either. Physical chores take real hours, and someone doing the bulk of the cooking and cleaning is doing a great deal even if their partner plans it.

Three practical ways to count both:

  • Add the planning as its own line. "Meal planning and shopping list" next to "cooking". "Tracking school emails" next to "school run".
  • Weight tasks that carry a lot of thinking. A task that needs constant monitoring, such as managing medication for an elderly parent, counts for more than its physical minutes.
  • Assign whole areas to one owner. When the same person anticipates and does the task, the split becomes much easier to see. Sharing cognitive labour step by step walks through how to do that.

EvenUS uses the first and third. Mental load is not a separate score, and it is not a multiplier on chores either. When does the gap between the two grow?

At life changes, when the amount of planning jumps faster than the amount of doing. A new baby is the clearest case. Night feeds and nappies can be shared fairly evenly, while vaccinations, weight checks, nursery waiting lists and the question of what the baby has eaten today all collect with the parent who was home more in the first months.

The same happens when both partners work full-time and the physical chores get outsourced or simplified. A cleaner reduces the physical work. Someone still has to find the cleaner, pay them, let them in and notice when they cancel. Outsourcing tends to turn physical work into cognitive work, and that new work lands with whoever arranged it.

How to estimate cognitive labour honestly

Use rough estimates, not a stopwatch, and estimate them together. Nobody can time thinking accurately, and trying to will make you both miserable.

A simple method: for each regular task, ask "how many times a week does someone have to think about this without being asked?" A task that needs thinking about once is light. A task that needs thinking about every day, like checking what is for dinner, is heavy even if each thought only takes a minute. Common questions

Is cognitive labour the same as emotional labour?

No. Cognitive labour is about logistics: what needs doing and when. Emotional labour is about managing feelings, such as keeping the peace or comforting someone, which is covered in emotional labour in relationships.

Which is harder, cognitive or physical work?

Neither is harder in general. Physical work takes more measurable time, while cognitive work is harder to switch off because it never quite finishes.

Can cognitive labour be outsourced?

Partly. Shared calendars and automatic bill payments remove some monitoring, but someone still has to set them up and notice when they go wrong.
