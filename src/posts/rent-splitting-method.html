Splitting rent 50/50 is fair when your incomes are close. When one partner earns noticeably more, a proportional split is usually fairer: each pays the same percentage of their take-home pay, so rent takes the same bite out of both incomes. If one of you has more private space, such as a home office, adjust for room size first, then split the rest.

Rent is usually the largest shared cost, and the one where the choice of method makes the biggest difference in pounds. It is also the one couples tend to settle in the estate agent's office, before anyone has thought about it. This article covers rent specifically. What is the difference between a 50/50 and a proportional rent split?

A 50/50 split gives each partner the same amount to pay. A proportional split gives each partner the same percentage of their income to pay. With equal incomes they are identical; with different incomes, they come apart quickly.

Sam takes home £3,000 a month and Alex £2,000. Their rent is £1,500.

50/50Proportional (60/40)
Sam pays£750£900
Alex pays£750£600
Rent as a share of Sam's pay25%30%
Rent as a share of Alex's pay37.5%30%
Left after rent£2,250 / £1,250£2,100 / £1,400

The proportional figures come from each person's share of the combined £5,000: Sam £3,000 ÷ £5,000 = 60%, Alex 40%. So Sam pays £1,500 × 0.6 = £900, and Alex £1,500 × 0.4 = £600.

Under 50/50, Alex spends well over a third of their pay on rent before any other bill. Under proportional, both spend 30%. That is the whole argument in one row.

When is splitting rent 50/50 fair?

When your incomes are within roughly 10 to 15% of each other, or when the rent is comfortably affordable for both of you. In those cases the difference between methods is small and the simplicity of equal shares is worth more.

Priya and Tom take home £2,600 and £2,400, with rent of £1,200. Equal shares are £600 each. Proportional would be £624 and £576, a difference of £24 a month. Either is fine. Some couples also prefer 50/50 as a matter of principle when both are financially independent, which is a legitimate choice as long as both genuinely can afford it.

When is a proportional rent split fairer?

When the gap is large enough that equal shares leave one person stretched while the other is comfortable. In practice, that is when one of you would be paying a clearly bigger fraction of your income for the same home.

There is a second reason that is easy to miss. With a big income gap, the flat you choose is often set by the higher earner's budget. If the higher earner wants the nicer area and the second bedroom, a 50/50 split asks the lower earner to stretch for a choice they would not have made alone. Proportional splitting, or the higher earner covering the extra, solves that. How do you split rent by room size?

Work out a cost per square metre, charge private rooms to whoever uses them, and split the shared space between the people living there. It sounds elaborate, but it takes five minutes with the floor plan from the listing.

For a couple: one partner has a home office

Sam works from home full time and uses the second bedroom as an office. The flat is 50 m² and the rent £1,500, so space costs £1,500 ÷ 50 = £30 per m². The office is 10 m², so it accounts for £300 of the rent.

  1. Office: £300, charged to Sam.
  2. Everything else: £1,500 − £300 = £1,200, split 60/40. Sam £720, Alex £480.
  3. Totals: Sam £720 + £300 = £1,020. Alex £480. Check: £1,020 + £480 = £1,500.

Whether to do this at all is a judgement call. Plenty of couples treat the office as shared space: Sam is saving on commuting, the room doubles as a guest room, and the household benefits from Sam's income. If Sam's employer pays a home-working allowance, that tips it towards charging the office to Sam. Talk it through once and write down what you decided.

For a couple sharing with a flatmate

Sam and Alex share a three-person flat with a friend, Jordan. The flat is 60 m² and the rent £1,800, which is again £30 per m².

  • Couple's bedroom: 16 m² × £30 = £480
  • Jordan's bedroom: 10 m² × £30 = £300
  • Shared space: 60 − 16 − 10 = 34 m² × £30 = £1,020, split three ways = £340 per person

The couple pays £480 + £340 + £340 = £1,160. Jordan pays £300 + £340 = £640. Check: £1,160 + £640 = £1,800. Sam and Alex then split their £1,160 between them, proportionally that is £696 and £464.

Charging shared space per person rather than per room is what makes this fair to the flatmate: two people use the kitchen and bathroom more than one does.

Should rent be split differently from other bills?

You can, and some couples do, but one method for all shared costs is simpler and causes fewer disputes. A common variation is splitting rent proportionally and smaller bills 50/50, on the basis that rent is where the gap actually bites.

If you mix methods, write down which cost uses which. Mixed systems that live only in memory tend to become arguments. A simple family ledger keeps track of who paid what and makes the monthly settle-up a single transfer.

What if one partner owns the home?

Then the other partner is not really splitting rent, and paying half the mortgage is usually not the right comparison. Mortgage payments build the owner's equity, so a partner who pays half without any share in the property is contributing to an asset that is not theirs.

Common approaches are for the non-owner to pay a fair share of running costs (bills, council tax, food) plus a contribution below the local market rent, or to agree a formal share in the property. The rights of unmarried partners in a home owned by one of them vary by country and are often weaker than people assume, so before money goes into a property you do not own, take advice from a solicitor or qualified financial adviser. Common questions

Who should pay the deposit when moving in together?

Many couples split the deposit in the same ratio as the rent, so each gets back what they put in. Write down who contributed what, so there is no guesswork if you move out or separate.

What if only one of us is on the tenancy agreement?

Usually the named tenant is the person the landlord can hold responsible for the rent, so check your agreement. Agree your split in writing between yourselves, and consider asking to add the other partner to the tenancy.

How do you split rent fairly with a big income gap?

Proportional is the usual starting point. If the lower earner is still left with very little after rent and bills, protect a personal allowance for both first and split the rest, as the household fairness calculator lets you test.
