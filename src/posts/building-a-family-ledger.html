A family ledger is a simple shared record of household spending: the date, what was bought, how much it cost, and which partner paid. Once a month you total it, compare what each of you paid with your agreed split, and one person transfers the difference. It tracks shared costs only, not personal spending. It takes an evening to set up and a few seconds per entry.

None of this is accounting. It is replacing two competing memories of who paid for what with one record you both trust. Most "I always end up paying" arguments are about that gap, and a ledger closes it either way: sometimes the feeling turns out to be accurate, sometimes it does not.

What goes in a family ledger?

Only costs the household shares: the things you both use or consume. Everything personal stays out, because the ledger is a record of the joint account, not a window into each other's lives.

In the ledgerOut of the ledger
Rent or mortgageYour own clothes, haircuts, gym
Council tax, energy, water, broadbandPersonal phone contract
Groceries and household suppliesLunches at work, coffees
Home insurance, repairs, servicingHobbies and subscriptions only one of you uses
Shared subscriptions, joint giftsGifts you give personally
Children's costs, if they are your shared childrenPersonal debt repayments

Agree the grey areas once, then stop debating them. Meals out together, the car if only one of you drives it to work, and pet costs are the usual candidates. Writing down the rule ("meals out together go in the ledger; takeaway for one does not") saves a dozen small conversations later. How do you set up a family ledger?

Choose a tool you will both actually open, set up five columns, and agree when you will settle. That is all the set-up there is.

  1. Pick the tool. A shared spreadsheet, a shared note on both phones, or an app that handles shared expenses. The best tool is the one with the least friction for whichever of you is less keen.
  2. Create the columns: date, item, amount, paid by, and a notes column for anything unusual.
  3. Write your split at the top. "Shared costs split 60/40 (Priya/Tom), based on take-home pay. Reviewed January 2027." If you have not chosen a split yet, how to split bills when you earn different amounts compares the options.
  4. Add the fixed costs first. Rent, bills and subscriptions go in at the start of each month, so only the variable spending needs logging as it happens.
  5. Agree the settle-up day. The first Sunday of the month works for many couples. Put it in both calendars.

If you have a joint account that all shared bills come from, the ledger gets shorter: it only needs the shared costs paid from personal accounts, such as the supermarket shop one of you did on your own card.

How do you settle up at the end of the month?

Add up what each person paid, work out what each should have paid under your split, and the difference is the transfer. One transfer, once a month.

Now the four lines of arithmetic:

  1. Priya paid: £1,200 + £35 + £64 + £71 = £1,370.
  2. Tom paid: £140 + £160 + £85 + £92 + £95 + £78 = £650.
  3. Total shared costs: £1,370 + £650 = £2,020. Priya's 60% share is £1,212. Tom's 40% share is £808.
  4. Difference: Priya paid £158 more than her share (£1,370 − £1,212). Tom paid £158 less than his (£808 − £650). Tom transfers £158 to Priya.

Both now sit exactly at their agreed share for October. Note the transfer in the ledger, and November starts from zero. If you split 50/50 instead, the method is identical with 50% in step 3. The formula for working out your own percentages is in income-proportional bill splitting.

What are the common mistakes with a shared ledger?

Logging personal spending, settling every transaction, and using the record as evidence. All three turn a useful tool into a source of friction.

Tracking too much

Once personal spending creeps in, the ledger becomes surveillance. Each of you should have money that is entirely yours and never appears anywhere the other can see.

Settling too often

Transferring £4.50 after every coffee run makes the relationship feel transactional. Monthly is the right rhythm for most couples; fortnightly at most.

Logging late

Entries made three weeks later are guesses. Log at the till or the same evening. A ledger with gaps produces arguments about the gaps.

Using it to keep score

The ledger records money, not virtue. If one of you starts quoting it in unrelated arguments, step back and talk about what is actually going on. The "who did more?" trap describes how this goes wrong.

Is a family ledger the same as a budget?

No. A budget is a plan for what you will spend; a ledger is a record of what you did spend and who paid. They work well together, but you can keep a ledger without a budget, and many couples start there.

After two or three months, the ledger becomes the raw material for a budget. You will see what groceries really cost, which bills arrive when, and where the surprises come from. That is a better starting point than estimates, and budgeting for couples takes it from there.

Why does a shared record help a relationship?

Because it turns a disagreement about memory into a fact you can both see. The partner who pays the big bills remembers the rent. The partner who does the weekly shop remembers dozens of smaller payments. Both are right about their own experience and wrong about the total.

A ledger also builds a habit of financial transparency without anyone giving up their privacy. You share the household's picture, not your personal one. Common questions

Is a spreadsheet good enough for a family ledger?

Yes, if both of you will update it. Spreadsheets tend to fail when only one person maintains them, because that person quietly becomes the household's bookkeeper. If that starts to happen, a tool that is easier to update on a phone may help.

What if one of us forgets to log things?

Add the fixed costs automatically at the start of the month and only log variable spending. If entries still go missing, check bank statements together at the monthly settle-up rather than chasing each other during the month.

Should unmarried couples keep a family ledger?

It is arguably more useful for unmarried couples, since there is often no joint account and a clear record of shared costs helps if you ever need to untangle things. The method is exactly the same.
