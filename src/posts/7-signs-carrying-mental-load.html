The clearest signs you carry most of the mental load at home are these: everyone asks you where things are, tasks only happen if you mention them, you cannot switch off in the evening, and handing a job over feels like more work than doing it. One sign on its own means little. Three or more together usually point to an imbalance worth raising.

The mental load is the planning, remembering and tracking behind running a home. Because it leaves no visible trace, the person carrying it often feels tired without being able to say why. These seven signs help you put a name to it.

1. You are the household search engine

If people in your home ask you, by default, where things are and when things happen, you are holding the information map. "Where is the passport?" "When is the parents' evening?" "Do we have any batteries?" Each question takes seconds. The fact that you can answer all of them means you are storing all of them.

Try counting for one day. If you field ten or more of these questions and your partner fields one or two, the gap is the load.

2. Things get done when you ask, and not before

If your partner helps willingly but only once prompted, the noticing still sits with you. On a Thursday evening, Maya says, "The bins go out tomorrow." Jordan takes them out straight away, cheerfully. The task was shared. The remembering was not.

This is the most common sign and the easiest to misread, because it looks like teamwork. It feels fine for the person who is asked. For the person asking, it is one more thing to track.

3. Delegating costs more than doing

If explaining a job takes longer than the job itself, you will keep doing it yourself, and the load stays put. Booking a child's dentist appointment takes five minutes. Explaining which dentist, which child, which days work, where the card is and what to say about the filling takes ten.

Over time, "it is easier if I just do it" becomes the default answer, and the other partner never builds the knowledge to take it over. Read the one who books everything if appointments and bookings are the bulk of your load.

4. You cannot properly switch off

If sitting down on the sofa does not stop the list running in your head, you are doing monitoring work. You remember the permission slip during a film. You wake at 3am thinking about the car insurance. Your partner, sitting next to you, is genuinely off duty.

This is often the sign people notice first, because it eats into rest. It is also the one most likely to lead to longer-term exhaustion if nothing changes.

5. You make most of the small decisions

If you decide what is for dinner, which present to buy, and whether the leak needs a plumber, you are carrying decision load. Each choice is minor. Together, they add up to decision fatigue, which is why choosing between pasta and rice at 6pm can feel unreasonably hard.

A good test: when your partner asks "what do you want to do about…?", do you usually end up deciding?

6. You are the default contact for school, family and services

If the school, the GP, the in-laws and the landlord all contact you first, you are the household's point of contact. Even when the message is about something your partner would happily handle, it lands with you, and so does the job of passing it on.

This often starts by accident: one name went on the school form first. Changing the contact details is a small, practical step that moves real load.

7. You feel like the nag

If reminding your partner about things makes you feel like a nag, that is usually a sign you are holding the list, not a sign that you are unreasonable. Nagging is what management looks like from the outside when it has not been agreed. Nobody wants to do it and nobody likes receiving it.

Both of you end up uncomfortable: one feels like a supervisor, the other feels supervised. The fix is not to remind more nicely. It is for the reminding to become unnecessary because the area belongs to someone else.

How many of these signs is a problem?

Use this as a rough guide, not a diagnosis. Tick each sign that is true most weeks.

Signs tickedWhat it likely means
0 to 1The load is probably shared reasonably. A light check-in every few months is enough.
2 to 3One or two areas have drifted to you. Moving one whole area would make a noticeable difference.
4 or moreYou are likely running the household. Worth a calm, specific conversation soon, before it turns into resentment.

Ask your partner to take the same test about themselves. If you both tick very different numbers, that difference is a good place to start the conversation. Different views of the same week are normal, and neither is a lie.

What should you do if you recognise these signs?

Make the load visible, then move one whole area to your partner. In that order.

  1. List it. Write down everything you track for one week. The mental load checklist by domain makes this quicker.
  2. Talk about the pattern, not the person. "I have been the contact for school all year" is easier to hear than "you never do anything". How to explain the mental load to a partner has wording that helps.
  3. Hand over one area completely. Including the remembering. Not "help with dinner" but "dinner is yours on weekdays".
  4. Check in weekly. Ten minutes to catch anything sliding back.

Common questions

Can men carry the mental load too?

Yes. Research on heterosexual couples finds women do more of it on average, but plenty of men carry most of it, and it shows up in same-sex couples too. The signs are the same whoever holds them.

Is carrying the mental load the same as being controlling?

Not usually. Most people who carry it would happily hand it over. The difference shows when an area is offered: someone carrying the load lets go, even if it feels uncomfortable at first.

Why do I feel guilty for complaining about the mental load?

Because each individual task looks tiny, so naming any one of them feels petty. The weight is in the total, which is why listing it all out tends to ease the guilt.
