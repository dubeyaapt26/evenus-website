The Fair Play method, from Eve Rodsky's 2019 book, is a card-based system: each household task is a card, and partners deal the cards between them so whoever holds a card owns that task completely, thinking included. Chore apps track and remind. Fair Play is better at the conversation and at handing over whole responsibilities. Apps are better at keeping it going and showing whether it stays fair. Many couples get the most from using both.

This is a comparison of approaches rather than a verdict. Both have real strengths, and the right choice depends on what has gone wrong in your household.

What is the Fair Play method?

Fair Play turns the work of running a home into a deck of cards, one per task or area, and asks a couple to sit down and decide who holds each card.

The central idea is ownership. Holding a card does not mean doing the task when reminded. It means being responsible for all of it: noticing it needs doing, planning it and carrying it out. If you hold the card for a child's dentist appointments, you remember the check-up is due, you book it, you put it in the diary, and you take them. Nobody has to ask.

That addresses one of the most common sources of friction between partners: one person managing and the other helping. Help that has to be requested still leaves the planning with the person asking. Why asking for help is part of the problem explains that pattern, and what the mental load is explains the planning work it leaves behind.

What does Fair Play do well?

It makes the invisible work visible, it forces a real conversation, and it moves whole responsibilities rather than single jobs.

  • It shows the scale of the work. Laying out every card on the table is often the first time a couple sees everything it takes to run their home. That moment alone changes conversations.
  • It hands over the thinking. Because a card includes the noticing and planning, passing a card on actually lightens the load of the person who held it.
  • It needs no technology. No accounts, no logging, nothing to update. Some couples find that a relief.
  • It is built for a conversation. The dealing session is the point. It gives you a structured reason to talk about the whole arrangement at once.

Where does Fair Play struggle?

The cards describe the arrangement you agreed, but they do not show what is happening week to week, and they say nothing about paid work or money.

  • The first session takes real time. Working through a full deck is an evening or more, and it needs both partners willing to sit down for it.
  • The deal can drift. A new job, a baby or an illness changes everything, and the cards do not tell you when that has happened. Without a regular review, the old deal quietly stops matching reality.
  • Cards are not sized. Ten cards that are daily jobs and ten that come up twice a year are not the same week. You have to judge the weight yourselves.
  • Paid work and money sit outside it. A partner working fifty paid hours and one working twenty need different shares of the cards to end up equally free, and the deck does not do that arithmetic.

What do chore apps do differently?

Apps keep a record over time. The good ones also measure the result, so you can see whether the arrangement you agreed is still fair.

Chore apps vary widely. Cleaning planners schedule jobs room by room. Family apps assign chores to children. Flat-share apps run rotas. Couples fairness apps such as EvenUS measure how time, money and mental load are shared. The best chore tracking apps sorts them by type.

Where apps are weaker is the conversation. An app can tell you there is a gap. It cannot sit you both down and make you look at the whole picture at once the way spreading out a deck of cards does. And a badly designed app can turn housework into a scoreboard, with points, streaks and reminders that read like nagging.

Which is better for couples?

Fair Play is the better starting point if you have never talked properly about who does what. An app is better if you have agreed a split but are not sure it is holding.

Maya and Jordan are a typical case for using both. They dealt the cards one Sunday afternoon. Maya passed Jordan the cards for school admin, meal planning and the car. Three months later, Maya felt the load had crept back. Jordan felt he was doing much more than before. Both were right. Jordan was cooking every weeknight, but a second baby had arrived, and night feeds and appointments had landed with Maya without any card being dealt. The cards were still accurate. The week had changed around them.

An app that measures free time each week would have shown the shift within a fortnight. A second dealing session would have fixed it. That is the honest division of labour between the two approaches: cards to decide, measurement to check.

How to combine Fair Play with an app

  1. Deal first. Do the conversation on paper or with the cards, with no phones. Decide who owns each area outright.
  2. Enter the outcome as owners, not jobs. In an app, each task should have one owner who is responsible for the whole thing, matching the card.
  3. Include the thinking tasks. Meal planning, tracking school dates and remembering birthdays should be tasks in their own right.
  4. Check monthly, re-deal after big changes. If free time drifts apart, deal again rather than adding more reminders.

EvenUS fits step two to four: each chore has one owner, mental-load tasks are included in its 29 starter chores, and each week it suggests one specific swap based on how free time is actually falling. It does not nudge your partner or let either of you mark the other's entries as wrong, which keeps the ownership principle intact. Moving towards proactive partnering covers the shift from helping to owning, whichever tool you use.

Common questions

Who created Fair Play?

Eve Rodsky, in her 2019 book Fair Play. It sets out the card-based system for dividing household tasks between partners.

Does Fair Play work for same-sex couples?

Yes. The method is about who owns which task, not about gender, so it works for any couple who share a home.

How long does it take to set up Fair Play?

Expect the first dealing session to take an evening or longer, depending on how many areas apply to your household. Later reviews are much quicker.

Can you use Fair Play without the cards?

Many couples use the idea with a simple list of areas and an owner for each. The ownership principle matters more than the physical cards.
