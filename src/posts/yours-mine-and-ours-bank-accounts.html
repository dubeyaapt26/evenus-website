The "yours, mine and ours" method gives a couple three accounts: one personal account each, and one joint account for shared costs. Each partner pays an agreed amount into the joint account every month, usually in proportion to income, and all bills, groceries and shared savings come out of it. What is left in each personal account belongs to that person, with no need to explain it. It sits between fully joint and fully separate finances, and for many couples it ends the "can I spend this?" conversation.

The three accounts are only plumbing. What makes the method work is agreeing what goes in "ours", how much each person contributes, and how you will both see the picture.

Joint vs separate accounts: which is better?

Neither is better in general. Each one solves a different problem and creates another. The three-account method is popular because it keeps the main benefit of each.

What goes in the "ours" account?

Anything the household consumes, rather than either person. If you would both lose out when it is not paid, it belongs in "ours".

  • Rent or mortgage, council tax or property tax
  • Energy, water, broadband, home insurance
  • Groceries and household supplies
  • Childcare, school costs and children's clothes
  • Shared subscriptions and the family car
  • Shared savings goals, such as a holiday or a house deposit
  • A small buffer for surprises

What stays in "yours" and "mine": clothes, hobbies, lunches out, gifts for each other, personal subscriptions, your own savings and pension top-ups. Phones are an edge case. Decide once and write it down.

How much should each partner put in? A worked example

Total your shared costs, then divide them in proportion to income. This keeps the share each of you gives up equal, even when one earns much more.

Nina takes home £2,800 a month and Omar £4,200. Together that is £7,000, so Nina earns 40% of the household income and Omar 60%.

Their "ours" budget:

Shared costMonthly
Rent£1,500
Council tax, energy, water, broadband£380
Groceries and household£520
Buffer£50
Joint savings (holiday and emergency fund)£350
Total into "ours"£2,800
  1. Nina's contribution: 40% of £2,800 is £1,120.
  2. Omar's contribution: 60% of £2,800 is £1,680.
  3. Left in "yours" and "mine": Nina keeps £1,680 and Omar keeps £2,520.

Both have put in exactly 40% of their pay. Compare a 50/50 split of £1,400 each. Nina would keep £1,400 and Omar £2,800, so Omar would have twice her personal money while both "paid half". The table looks fair and the leftovers do not.

Proportional is a sensible default, but not the only option. If one partner's income is low, protecting a personal allowance first (equal remainder) or leaving both with the same amount (residual) may suit you better. All four methods are worked through in how to split bills when you earn different amounts.

How do you set up the three accounts?

Open the joint account, fund it by standing order the day after payday, and move every shared bill onto it. Step by step:

  1. List every shared cost from three months of statements, plus irregular ones like car insurance.
  2. Agree a split method and calculate each contribution.
  3. Open a joint current account. Add a joint savings pot if you have shared goals.
  4. Set up one standing order from each personal account into the joint account.
  5. Move direct debits for shared bills to the joint account.
  6. Give both of you a card and full online access to the joint account.
  7. Agree a date to review the amounts, at least once a year and whenever an income changes.

The timing details, sinking funds for irregular costs, and how to stop bills leaving before the money arrives are covered in how to automate your household spending.

What are the risks of a joint account?

Mainly shared liability and shared credit records, so know them before you open one. With most joint accounts, either holder can usually withdraw the full balance, and both can be responsible for an overdraft. In some countries, including the UK, a joint account can also link your credit files, so one partner's credit history may affect the other's applications.

Ask your bank exactly how its joint accounts work. If either of you has significant debt, get advice from a free debt charity before opening anything joint. And keep a personal account in your own name whatever you decide. Everyone should have money of their own that they can reach on their own.

Why the method also cuts the mental load

It moves the remembering off one person. Without a shared account, someone has to track who paid the energy bill, work out who owes whom, and raise it. That is planning and monitoring work, and it nearly always settles on the same partner. With an "ours" account funded automatically, most of that disappears. What remains, the annual review and the occasional new bill, can be given a named owner rather than drifting.

Personal accounts help with something quieter too: neither person has to justify a purchase. Agree a threshold for mentioning big personal spends, perhaps anything over £300, and leave everything below it alone. Common questions

Should married couples have separate bank accounts?

Many do, and it is a perfectly healthy setup. Marriage does not require merged finances. What matters is agreeing the shared costs and both being able to see them.

What happens to the joint account if we break up?

Rules vary by bank and country, and either holder may be able to withdraw funds or freeze the account. Check your bank's terms now, and get legal advice if you separate.

Should savings go in the joint account or personal accounts?

Shared goals, like a holiday or an emergency fund, belong in a joint savings pot. Personal goals and pensions belong in your own name. Keeping them apart makes it clear whose money is whose.

How often should we change the contributions?

Whenever either income changes, and at least once a year. Prices rise, so check the "ours" budget at the same time.
