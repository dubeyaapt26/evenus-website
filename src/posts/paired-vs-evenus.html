Paired is a daily-questions app built to help couples talk to each other. EvenUS measures how the household work is divided and suggests one thing to change. They address different complaints, and the useful question is which complaint you have.

As with any comparison of live products: this is about the two approaches, not a feature table. Apps ship changes monthly and a table written today would be quietly wrong by spring. Check Paired's current app for what it does now. What follows is what each is for, which does not move.

What a connection app does

Paired and the category it belongs to work on the relationship itself. A question a day that both of you answer, conversation prompts, quizzes, small exercises that get two people talking about something other than logistics.

The problem it addresses is drift. Two people who live together, coordinate constantly, and have not had a conversation that was not admin in several months. The intervention is deliberately light: a small prompt, daily, that costs almost nothing and reopens a channel.

That is a real problem and the format suits it. If your complaint is "we never really talk any more", this is the right category and nothing here is going to argue otherwise.

What EvenUS does instead

EvenUS does not work on the relationship. It measures the arrangement.

It calculates each partner's discretionary hours, meaning what is left of the week once sleep, paid work, commuting and household work come out. It weights planning-heavy work above execution-heavy work of the same length. It tracks money on a separate axis and never converts hours into currency. Then once a week it names one specific thing to swap, and why now.

The complaint it addresses is not distance. It is one person feeling they carry most of the running of the house while the other cannot see what they mean.

The difference that matters

A conversation prompt improves how you talk about a problem. It does not tell you what the problem is, and for this particular problem the two of you disagree about the facts.

That is the specific difficulty with an uneven household. It is not usually a communication breakdown. Both people can be articulate, willing and kind, and still be unable to resolve it, because one of them is describing work the other has never seen happen. Better conversational technique applied to two incompatible accounts of the week produces a better-tempered stalemate.

What breaks that deadlock is a shared picture, arrived at separately, that neither person is arguing with the other about. Then the conversation has somewhere to start. The mental load is about why the invisible half is so hard to describe from the inside.

Which do you need?

A rough test, and it takes a minute.

Ask yourselves separately what the last three arguments were actually about. If they were about tone, attention, or feeling unheard, a connection app is aimed at your problem. If they were about the dishes, the shop, whose turn it was, or who booked the appointment, they were about division, and no amount of daily questions will settle who does the shop.

The other test: ask each of you, separately, roughly what share of the household running you do. Two close answers mean you agree on the facts and the issue is elsewhere. Fifty against eighty means you do not agree on the facts, and that is the thing to fix first.

They are also not alternatives to each other in any real sense. A daily question and a fairness measure barely overlap, and using both is a perfectly sensible thing to do.

Where each one stops

A connection app stops at the point where the problem is structural. If one person is doing eleven more hours a week, the honest answer is not a better conversation about it, it is eleven fewer hours. Prompts can make that discussion kinder. They cannot make it unnecessary, and a couple who have talked warmly about the same imbalance for a year usually feel worse rather than better.

EvenUS stops at everything that is not the arrangement. It has nothing to say about intimacy, attention, how you speak to each other, or whether you are still interested in one another's days. Those matter enormously and this is not a tool for them.

It is also not therapy and does not pretend to be. If the picture stays bad for a couple of months it stops suggesting things, says so once, and points toward someone qualified.

What EvenUS deliberately will not do

Worth listing, because some of it is what people expect from a couples app.

  • No prompts, streaks or daily engagement mechanics. A streak punishes whoever had a hard week, and an app in this territory should not be optimising for how often you open it.
  • No nudging your partner. A reminder attaches to a task and reaches only whoever owns it, never labelled with who set it.
  • No score shown for the other person. The output describes the arrangement, not either of you.
  • Answers stay private. The weekly check-in is not readable by your partner, and that is enforced in the database rather than hidden in the interface.

The honest caveats

Paired is a shipped product you can install this afternoon. EvenUS is in closed testing and not yet on the app stores. That is a real difference and it is not in our favour.

EvenUS needs both of you. It compares two people, so one person using it alone gets very little out of it. A daily-question app degrades more gracefully when only one partner is enthusiastic, which is a genuine advantage.

It asks about a week, not a moment. Roughly thirty seconds each, once a week. If you want something that gives you a small thing to do every day, that is not the shape of this.

If you are not sure

The cheapest way to find out which problem you have is to look at the numbers before installing anything. The fairness calculator runs the discretionary-hours arithmetic in a browser with no account.

If the gap comes back small, your issue is probably not division and a connection app is the better spend of your attention. If it comes back large, that is worth addressing directly, because an imbalance left running tends to surface later as the thing a connection app is trying to repair.
