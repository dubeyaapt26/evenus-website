Why is the mental load hard to track?

Because most of it happens in your head, in small pieces, while you are doing something else.

Noticing the milk is low takes two seconds. Remembering that the car insurance renews next month takes no visible time at all. Neither shows up on a chore list, and neither feels worth writing down. But together, across a household, they add up to a constant background job. The sociologist Allison Daminger, in a 2019 paper in the American Sociological Review, described this cognitive labour as anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding between them, and monitoring the results. Only the last step, if that, ever looks like a task.

That is why counting completed chores misses it, and why simply asking "what do you do around here?" produces two very different answers. What the mental load is covers the concept in full. This article is about measuring it.

How do you track mental load without an app?

Use three steps: a noticing log for one week, a list of areas with owners, and a private comparison.

Step 1: Keep a noticing log for one week

Each of you keeps a note on your phone. Every time you notice, remember, plan or decide something for the household, write three or four words. "Kids need new school shoes." "Book boiler service." "What's for dinner Thursday." Do not record time. Just the item.

On Sunday, compare the length of the lists. You are not looking for a winner. You are looking for how much of the household's thinking passed through each of you. Often one list is two or three times the length of the other, and the shorter one belongs to someone who is genuinely surprised.

Step 2: Turn the log into areas

Group the items into areas: meal planning, groceries, home upkeep, money admin, the social calendar, gifts and occasions, travel, insurance and paperwork, school admin, children's health, children's activities, pets, caring for parents, the car. Cross out any that do not apply to you.

Step 3: Mark owners separately

Each of you, without looking at the other's, marks every area as mine, shared, my partner's, or someone we pay. Then compare. Where you agree, fine. Where you disagree, you have found the invisible part.

The mental load checklist for couples gives a fuller list of tasks if you want a prompt for step one.

How does EvenUS track mental load?

In three ways: planning tasks are chores with their own owner, a short set of private questions records who holds each area, and planning is counted as hours on top of the hours spent doing. Mental load is not a separate score or a multiplier. Each area carries an estimated number of hours a week, so the planning counts when free time is added up.

1. Planning tasks as chores

EvenUS comes with 29 starter chores, and several are pure mental-load tasks: plan the week's meals, write the grocery list, track school dates and forms, keep track of what needs replacing, remember birthdays and buy gifts, renew subscriptions and insurance. Each chore has one owner, a usual time and a frequency. If something in your household is missing, add it. How to add chores, bills and mental-load tasks walks through it.

2. Who holds each area, answered privately

There is a short set of questions, one area at a time, taking around three minutes: meal planning, groceries, home upkeep, money admin and so on, with children, pets, caring for parents and the car included where they apply. For each, you choose me, shared, them, or someone we pay. You each answer separately, and neither of you sees the other's answers until you have both finished. There is no editing yours after seeing theirs.

Once you have both answered, each area's estimated hours a week (from half an hour to three hours) are shared out according to your answers and added to each person's time. Where your answers differ, the app shows that you see the area differently. It does not say who is right. A gap in perception is not a lie on either side. It usually means the planning is happening somewhere one partner cannot see.

3. The doing, logged as the time it took

There is a weekly catch-up screen for the chores. Recurring chores are already ticked against their usual owner, so most weeks you untick what did not happen, change who did it where needed, set how long your own really took, and tap Log these. Only logged time counts towards the week, never the chore list's estimate. The planning side needs no logging at all: it comes from the area answers above.

What should you not try to track?

Emotional labour, minute-by-minute time, and anything that turns into keeping score.

  • Emotional labour, such as managing moods, keeping the peace or supporting family, is real and important. EvenUS deliberately does not score it, because putting a number on care quickly feels transactional. Talk about it instead. Emotional labour vs mental load explains the difference.
  • Exact minutes of noticing. Nobody can log twenty seconds of noticing the milk is low. That is why the planning is counted by area, as estimated hours a week, rather than logged moment by moment.
  • Each other. Track your own work, not your partner's. In EvenUS neither of you can edit or dispute the other's entries, and there is no way to nudge your partner. A reminder belongs to a chore and goes only to its owner.

What to do once you have the data

Look at the pattern after three or four weeks, then change one thing. One week is noise. A month shows where the planning reliably sits. Common questions

Is there an app to track mental load?

A few apps try. Most chore apps track completed tasks only. EvenUS treats planning as tasks with owners and asks both partners privately who holds each area of home life.

How long should you track mental load before talking about it?

A one-week noticing log is enough to start a conversation. For a reliable pattern, give it three or four weeks.

What if my partner won't track anything?

Start with your own one-week log and the list of areas, then ask them to mark owners separately. It takes a few minutes and often changes the conversation more than any amount of data.

Can you put a number on the mental load?

Only roughly. Estimates of planning time are useful for spotting patterns, but treat any figure as a conversation starter rather than a verdict.
