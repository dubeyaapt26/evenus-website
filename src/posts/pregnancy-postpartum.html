The postpartum mental load is all the tracking, planning and remembering a new baby brings: feeds, nappies, appointments, vaccinations, sleep, clothes sizes and what is running low. It tends to land on whoever is on leave. To share it, split it during pregnancy, keep the baby knowledge in one shared place, give each parent whole areas to own, and re-divide again when leave ends.

A new baby breaks almost every household arrangement, including good ones. That is not a failure of the arrangement. The workload grows enormously at the moment when both of you have the least sleep, and the default is set within weeks.

What is the postpartum mental load?

It is the thinking work of the first year, as opposed to the physical care. Changing a nappy is a task. Noticing that the nappies are nearly gone, knowing which size is next and remembering to order them is the mental load.

Examples many new parents recognise:

  • Tracking feeds, sleep and nappies, and knowing what is normal for this baby.
  • Booking and remembering health checks, vaccinations and registration deadlines.
  • Knowing which clothes fit, and when the next size is needed.
  • Researching every new question, from rashes to weaning.
  • Keeping visitors, gifts and thank-you messages organised.
  • Planning childcare and the return to work, often months ahead.

Allison Daminger's 2019 research on cognitive labour describes four parts of this kind of work: anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding, and monitoring. In the first year, all four run around the clock. What is the mental load? has the general version.

Why does it land on one parent, even in equal couples?

Because expertise becomes ownership by accident. The parent on leave spends every hour with the baby, so within a few weeks they simply know more. The other parent starts asking rather than deciding, and asking moves the thinking straight back.

Research on couples consistently finds that in heterosexual couples, women do more unpaid domestic work on average, and the arrival of a first child is often when that gap widens. But the mechanism is not really about gender. Same-sex couples see the same thing when one parent takes most of the leave. Whoever is home becomes the default parent, and the default is hard to undo later. Why mums carry most of the mental load looks at the gendered side of this.

There is also a quieter pattern. The parent who knows more may correct the other parent's way of doing things, and the other parent steps back. Both are reacting reasonably to tiredness. It still ends with one person holding everything.

How do you share the mental load during pregnancy?

Divide it before the birth, while you still have the sleep to think. Use the months beforehand to assign areas, not just tasks.

A pregnancy checklist for Hannah and Jess, who are expecting their first child and where Hannah is giving birth:

  1. Jess owns the logistics project: car seat, cot, pram, the nursery and the paperwork for leave.
  2. Hannah owns the birth plan and antenatal appointments, with Jess attending and keeping notes in a shared document.
  3. They split future areas now: Jess will own nappies, clothes and supplies. Hannah will own feeding. Both will share health appointments, alternating who books.
  4. Jess sorts the household running for the first three months: meal plan, online shop, cleaner or not, visitor rules.
  5. They agree a check-in every Sunday for ten minutes, starting before the birth.

The point is that the non-birthing parent takes whole areas early, so they are an owner from day one rather than a helper who arrives later.

How do you share the load in the first twelve weeks?

Make the information shared and the nights planned. Most of the arguments in this period are not about effort. They are about one person holding knowledge the other cannot access.

  • One shared record. Feeds, sleep, medicines and questions for the health visitor go in one note or app you can both see. Nobody should have to ask "when did she last feed?"
  • Nights in shifts. If feeding allows, split the night into two blocks so each person gets one stretch of unbroken sleep. If one parent is breastfeeding, the other can own everything around the feed: nappies, settling and the bottle washing.
  • Owned areas, not favours. "Supplies are yours" means the other parent never thinks about wipes. "Can you pick up wipes?" means they still do.
  • Lower the standards elsewhere. A tidy kitchen is optional this quarter. Say so out loud so nobody silently carries it.

The new parents guide covers day-to-day routines, and sharing cognitive labour has a step-by-step method for passing on the thinking, not just the tasks.

What happens when parental leave ends?

Re-divide from scratch, because the parent going back to work cannot keep everything they held at home. This is the second big reset of the first year, and it often goes worse than the first because nobody plans for it.

About a month before the return, list everything the parent on leave has been running and decide which areas move. Childcare drop-offs, nursery communication and sick-day cover are the big new ones. Decide in advance who takes the first sick day, because there will be one in the first fortnight. Dividing housework without an argument explains why handing over whole areas holds up.

How do you split money in the first year?

Revisit your split, because leave pay is usually lower and the baby adds costs. A 50/50 split that worked before can leave the parent on leave with almost nothing of their own.

Say Hannah's take-home pay drops from £2,600 to £1,200 a month on leave, and Jess takes home £2,800. Shared costs rise to £2,500.

  • Equal: £1,250 each. Hannah is left with minus £50.
  • Proportional: Jess has 70% of the income, so pays £1,750 and Hannah pays £750. Jess keeps £1,050 and Hannah keeps £450.

Proportional or equal remainder splits usually fit this year far better. How to split bills when you earn different amounts shows all four methods.

When it is more than tiredness

Exhaustion is normal. Persistent low mood, anxiety, intrusive thoughts or feeling unable to cope are not something to manage with a better rota. Postnatal depression and anxiety can affect either parent, including the one who did not give birth. Talk to your GP, midwife or health visitor, and if anyone is at risk, contact emergency services.

EvenUS can help you see how the load is shared once things settle, and it flags when both of you are overloaded rather than calling that a balanced week. It does not treat anything, and it is not a substitute for medical support.

Common questions

How long does the postpartum period last for the mental load?

The medical postpartum period is usually described as the first weeks after birth, but the household reset tends to last the whole first year, including the return to work.

What can the non-birthing parent take on if the baby is breastfed?

Plenty: nappies, settling, supplies, appointments, household running, visitors and all the night-time tasks around each feed. Owning those areas outright makes a real difference.

Is it normal to argue more after having a baby?

Many couples find the first year strained, mostly from lack of sleep and a sudden jump in work. If arguments become constant or frightening, speak to a counsellor or your GP.

Should we split night feeds 50/50?

Not necessarily. Aim for each parent to get one protected block of sleep, and balance the rest across the day.
