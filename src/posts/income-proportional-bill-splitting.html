Income-proportional bill splitting means each partner pays the same percentage of their income towards shared costs, rather than the same amount. The formula is simple: your share of the bills equals your income divided by your combined income. If you bring home 70% of the household's money, you pay 70% of the rent, food and bills. Both of you then feel the household's costs to the same degree.

This article is the working version: the formula, a calculation you can copy line by line, and what to do when one income moves around from month to month. If you are still deciding whether proportional is the right method at all, the comparison of the four ways to split bills when you earn different amounts runs the same numbers through each one.

What is the formula for proportional bill splitting?

Your share = your income ÷ (your income + your partner's income) × total shared costs. That is the whole formula. Everything else is deciding which income figure and which costs to put into it.

Written another way, there are two steps. First work out each person's percentage of the combined income. Then apply that percentage to every shared bill, or to the monthly total.

The useful property is that it holds steady as costs change. If the utility bill goes up by $60, the higher earner absorbs a bigger slice of the increase automatically, and nobody has to renegotiate.

How do you calculate a proportional split step by step?

Six steps, and the arithmetic takes about ten minutes the first time. Here they are with Priya and Tom, who take home $5,600 and $2,400 a month.

  1. Agree the income figure. Use monthly take-home pay, after tax and compulsory deductions. Priya: $5,600. Tom: $2,400.
  2. Add them together. $5,600 + $2,400 = $8,000.
  3. Work out each percentage. Priya: $5,600 ÷ $8,000 = 0.70, so 70%. Tom: $2,400 ÷ $8,000 = 0.30, so 30%.
  4. Total the shared costs. Rent $2,100, utilities $350, groceries $700, internet and phone for the house $140, household odds $110. Total: $3,400.
  5. Apply the percentages. Priya: $3,400 × 0.70 = $2,380. Tom: $3,400 × 0.30 = $1,020. Check: $2,380 + $1,020 = $3,400.
  6. Check what is left. Priya: $5,600 − $2,380 = $3,220. Tom: $2,400 − $1,020 = $1,380.

Step six is the one people skip, and it is the one that tells you whether the split is livable. Both of them have spent 42.5% of their take-home pay on the household ($2,380 ÷ $5,600 and $1,020 ÷ $2,400 both come to 0.425). Tom's $1,380 is still much smaller than Priya's $3,220, which is how proportional splitting works: it equalizes the effort, not the outcome. If $1,380 is too tight for Tom to live on, that is a sign to look at the equal remainder method in the pillar article rather than to tweak the percentages by feel.

Should you round the percentages?

Yes, to the nearest whole number, and usually to the nearest five. The difference is small and a round number is easier to remember and to set up as a recurring transfer.

Sam and Alex take home $4,400 and $2,600. Combined: $7,000. Sam's exact share is $4,400 ÷ $7,000 = 0.6286, or 62.86%. Their shared costs are $3,000.

How do you split bills proportionally with irregular income?

Use an average to set a provisional split, then true it up at the end of each quarter against what was actually earned. This keeps the monthly payments predictable while making sure the split reflects reality over time.

Freelancers, commission earners, shift workers and the self-employed all have this problem. A split based on one good month makes the next lean month painful, and a split recalculated every month turns the household budget into a moving target.

Step 1: set the provisional split from a 12-month average

Maya is freelance. Over the last twelve months she took home $36,000 after setting aside tax, which averages $3,000 a month. Jordan is salaried at $4,500 a month take-home. Combined: $7,500. Provisional split: Maya 40%, Jordan 60%. Shared costs are $3,000 a month, so Maya pays $1,200 and Jordan $1,800.

Step 2: true it up each quarter

Then Maya has a slow quarter and earns only $4,500 across the three months. Jordan earns his usual $13,500. The real ratio for that quarter is $4,500 ÷ $18,000 = 25% for Maya and 75% for Jordan.

Other ways couples handle variable pay

  • Base salary only. Split on the guaranteed part of each income, and treat bonuses or commission separately, for example into joint savings in the same ratio.
  • A floor for lean months. Agree that neither person pays more than a fixed share of what they actually earned in a month, with the difference settled when income recovers.
  • A rolling three-month average. Recalculate every month using the last three months. More responsive, more admin.

Whichever you pick, a buffer helps. The partner with irregular income does better keeping a month or two of their share set aside, so a quiet month does not become a household conversation.

What income and costs go into the formula?

Most couples use take-home pay and costs the household consumes together. Gross income tends to overstate the higher earner's share, because tax takes a bigger bite at higher incomes. Personal spending such as your own phone, clothes and hobbies stays out.

The gray areas are real: retirement contributions, children from a previous relationship, a car one of you uses for work, debts that predate the relationship. They are covered in detail in what goes into a fair financial split, so this article does not repeat them.

How do you actually pay it each month?

Either both pay their percentage into a joint account, or one person pays the bills and the other settles up monthly. Both work. The method is the decision; the plumbing is convenience.

  • Joint bills account: Priya sets a recurring transfer for $2,380, Tom for $1,020, and every shared bill comes out of that account. Nothing to reconcile unless costs change.
  • Pay and settle: whoever happens to pay a bill pays it, both of you keep a simple record, and at the end of the month the person who paid less than their share transfers the difference. A family ledger is the easiest way to keep that record honest.

EvenUS uses proportional splitting as its default and tracks what each of you paid against what the split says, including settlements between you. The free household fairness calculator will run the formula on your own numbers without an account.

When should you recalculate?

Whenever either income changes by more than a small amount, and otherwise once a year. A pay rise, a new job, parental leave, a move to part-time, redundancy: each changes the ratio, and a split that is not updated slowly turns back into something nobody chose.

Put a date in the calendar. The annual check takes ten minutes with the steps above. The bigger moments deserve a proper conversation, and rebalancing after a job loss, promotion or relocation walks through those.

Common questions

Is proportional bill splitting fair to the higher earner?

It asks both people to spend the same share of their income on the household, and the higher earner still keeps more money in absolute terms. Most people find that fair; if the higher earner feels otherwise, the conversation is usually about decision-making rather than the percentage.

What if one partner earns nothing?

The formula gives them a 0% share, which is mathematically tidy and emotionally awkward. Couples in that position usually add a protected personal allowance for both people so the partner without income is not asking for every coffee.

Do we split savings proportionally too?

You can, and many couples do for joint goals such as a down payment on a house. Keep it as a separate decision from bills, because savings goals are about what you both want, not what the household consumes.

What happens to the split during parental leave?

The ratio changes as soon as one income drops, so recalculate for the months of leave rather than carrying on with the old numbers. Some couples switch to a protected personal allowance for both people during leave, since the drop in income comes from a decision you made together.
