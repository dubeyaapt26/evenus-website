A simple couples budget takes seven steps: add up both take-home incomes, list fixed shared costs, list variable shared costs, set shared goals, decide how to split the total, check what each of you has left as personal money, and review monthly. Budget only what is shared, use one agreed split method, and judge fairness by what each person has left, not by what each pays.

Below is the template as a table you can copy, then the same template filled in with a real-feeling example so you can see every number. It takes about an hour the first time and about twenty minutes each month after that.

The couples budget template

Work through the steps in order. Each one produces a single number that feeds the next.

The template filled in: Sam and Alex

Sam and Alex rent a flat together. They have separate current accounts and a shared one for bills.

Step 1: Total your take-home income

Use what actually lands in your accounts each month, not gross salary. Sam takes home £2,900 and Alex £2,100. Combined: £5,000.

If either income varies, use a cautious figure, such as the lowest month of the past six, and treat anything above it as a bonus to allocate when it arrives.

Check three months of statements so nothing is missed. Yearly bills paid monthly belong here. Yearly bills paid in one go belong in step 4.

Running costs so far: £1,700 + £790 = £2,490.

Step 4: Set shared goals

GoalMonthly
Emergency fund£300
Holiday£150
Annual costs pot (car service, gifts, TV licence)£100
Total goals£550

Total shared budget: £2,490 + £550 = £3,040.

The annual costs pot is the step most couples skip, and it is why so many budgets "fail" in December or when the car needs work. Add up the irregular yearly costs, divide by 12, and set that aside every month.

Step 5: Choose how to split it

Pick the method before you look at who pays which bill. Sam and Alex earn different amounts, so they split in proportion to income.

  • Sam's share of income: £2,900 ÷ £5,000 = 58%.
  • Alex's share of income: £2,100 ÷ £5,000 = 42%.
  • Sam contributes 58% of £3,040 = £1,763.20.
  • Alex contributes 42% of £3,040 = £1,276.80.

Check: £1,763.20 + £1,276.80 = £3,040. Each sets up a monthly transfer to the shared account for that amount, the day after payday.

Proportional is one of four sensible methods. How to split bills when you earn different amounts runs all four, and income proportional bill splitting goes deeper into this one.

Step 6: Check what each of you has left

  • Sam: £2,900 minus £1,763.20 = £1,136.80.
  • Alex: £2,100 minus £1,276.80 = £823.20.

Together that is £1,960, which matches £5,000 minus £3,040. This is personal money. Each person's own pension top-ups, clothes, hobbies, personal subscriptions and personal savings come from here, without needing the other's agreement.

For comparison, a 50/50 split would mean £1,520 each, leaving Sam with £1,380 and Alex with £580. That is the gap that tends to turn into tension over small purchases. If one partner's personal figure looks too thin under any method, consider protecting a fixed personal amount first. Managing household finances without losing yourself explains that approach.

Step 7: Review monthly, reset twice a year

A monthly check keeps the budget accurate. A reset every six months keeps it fair.

  • Monthly (20 minutes): compare actual shared spending with the plan, move any surplus to a goal, agree one change.
  • Twice a year: update incomes, bills and goals, and recalculate the split.
  • Straight away: after a pay rise, job loss, new baby or move.

Where do debts fit in a couples budget?

Minimum payments on shared or joint debts go in step 2, overpayments in step 4, and personal debts usually come out of personal money unless you agree otherwise. Agree this explicitly, because it changes how much each of you has left. Managing debt as a couple covers whose debt is whose and how to choose a payoff order. If repayments are already more than you can manage, get free debt advice before building the rest of the budget.

What makes a couples budget different from a personal one?

Two incomes, one set of shared costs, and a need to protect each person's independence. A personal budget asks "can I afford this?". A couples budget also asks "is this split fair?" and "does each of us still have money of our own?".

It also adds work. Someone has to track the bills, notice when a direct debit rises and run the monthly check. If that always falls to one person, it is worth sharing deliberately. Common questions

What is the 50/30/20 rule for couples?

It is a rule of thumb: about half of take-home pay on needs, 30% on wants and 20% on saving or debt. Couples can apply it to combined income, but it does not tell you how to split costs between you.

Should couples have a joint budget if they keep separate accounts?

Yes. The budget covers shared costs whatever accounts you use. Couple finances without a joint bank account shows how to run it.

How much should couples save each month?

There is no single right figure. Many start with an emergency fund of a few months of shared costs, then add goals once that is in place.

What if our budget does not balance?

Cut variable costs first, then goals, and look at fixed bills when contracts come up for renewal. If essentials alone exceed income, speak to a free debt or money advice service.
