The second shift is the unpaid household work that starts when the paid working day ends: cooking, cleaning, childcare and the planning that holds it together. Delegating chores to a partner backfires because delegation moves the doing but not the thinking. The person handing out jobs still notices what needs doing, decides who does it and checks it happened, so their second shift never actually gets shorter. What works instead is handing over whole areas of responsibility, including the planning.

What is the second shift?

The term comes from the sociologist Arlie Hochschild's 1989 book The Second Shift, which described the housework and childcare that working parents, mostly women, took on after a full day of paid work. Her point was that paid work had changed far faster than the division of work at home.

The pattern she described is still recognisable. Research on couples consistently finds that women in heterosexual relationships do more unpaid domestic work on average, even when both partners work full-time. The shape has shifted since 1989: many partners now do a substantial share of the physical tasks. What has moved more slowly is the part of the second shift that is hardest to see, the planning.

That invisible layer is now usually called the mental load. It is the reason the second shift can feel just as long in a household where the chores look evenly split.

Why does delegating chores not work?

Because the delegator stays the manager. When one person asks and the other does, the asking is a job in its own right, and it is often the heavier one.

Look at a single evening for Ellie and Marcus. Marcus is happy to help and does whatever he is asked. At 6pm, Ellie asks him to start dinner. He asks what they are having. She tells him. He asks where the rice is. She tells him. At 7pm she reminds him that their daughter has a spelling test tomorrow. At 8pm she asks him to put a wash on, and mentions that the swimming kit needs to be in it.

Marcus has done four jobs. Ellie has done four jobs too: she noticed each one, decided it needed doing, worked out the details and passed them on. She will also check, without meaning to, whether the swimming kit made it into the machine. Her second shift has not shrunk. It has become a management role on top of the work.

The sociologist Allison Daminger described this kind of cognitive labour in four stages: anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding, and monitoring. Delegation hands over only the carrying out, which comes after all four, and leaves every one of them exactly where it was. Cognitive labour versus physical chores goes into the difference in more depth.

Why does "just tell me what to do" make it worse?

Because telling someone what to do is the work. The offer is usually sincere, but it asks the partner who already carries the planning to keep carrying it and add a briefing on top.

It also puts the delegating partner in a role neither person wants. Every reminder starts to sound like nagging, and every question sounds like a test. Over time, the partner doing the asking feels like a supervisor, and the partner being asked feels supervised. Neither is a good basis for an evening together. The cartoonist Emma's 2017 comic "You Should've Asked" is still the clearest picture of this, and it is the comic that popularised the phrase "mental load". Why asking for help is part of the problem takes this further.

Delegating versus owning: what is the difference?

Delegating moves a task for one occasion. Owning moves a whole area for good, including the noticing and deciding. The table shows how the same area looks under each.

What to do instead of delegating

Hand over complete areas, one at a time, and let the new owner run them their way. These steps make the handover stick.

  1. Choose an area with a clear edge. Children's swimming. All weekday breakfasts. The car. Bins, recycling and anything to do with the council.
  2. Do one proper handover. Share everything the current owner knows in one sitting: dates, logins, preferences, where things live. After that, questions go to a shared note or are answered by the new owner's own judgement.
  3. Move the reminders. If calendar alerts, school emails or delivery texts come to the old owner, redirect them.
  4. Agree on standards in advance. What actually has to happen, and what is just how the old owner liked it.
  5. Leave it alone. No checking, no rescuing. If something slips, the new owner fixes it and learns the rhythm.

The hardest step is usually the last one, for the person letting go. Watching an area you have run for years being done differently is uncomfortable. But a handed-over area that keeps getting checked has not been handed over, and the second shift stays the same length.

How do you know whether your second shift is shrinking?

Look at free time and interruptions, not at a list of completed chores. A shorter second shift shows up as evenings that are genuinely off, not just a tidier kitchen.

  • Can the person who used to run everything sit down after dinner without a list running?
  • Do questions about the handed-over area still come to them?
  • Have their hours of genuinely free time gone up?

EvenUS measures this as discretionary time: the hours each partner has left after sleep, paid work, commuting and household work, with the planning each of you holds counted as hours too. It is one way to see whether a handover has actually changed anything. If you both work long hours, balancing the mental load when both partners work full-time has more on making room for this.

Common questions

Who coined the term "second shift"?

Arlie Hochschild, in her 1989 book The Second Shift, based on her research with working couples in the United States.

Is delegating chores always a bad idea?

Not for one-off jobs or when someone is ill or away. It becomes a problem when it is the permanent arrangement, because the person delegating never stops managing.

What if my partner forgets things once they own them?

Expect a few slips in the first month while they build their own system. If it keeps happening, look at whether the area is too big or whether the reminders were ever moved over.

Does the second shift apply to couples without children?

Yes. Children add a great deal of work, but cooking, cleaning, bills, family birthdays and home admin still make up a second shift in any shared home.
