Every couple needs a household COO because every household already has one: the person who plans, schedules, notices and keeps track of what the home needs. The problem is that the role is usually unnamed and falls to one partner by default. Naming it, writing down what it involves, and splitting or rotating it turns invisible work into a job you can share. The aim is not a boss. It is a clear owner for the running of each part of the house.

The "chief operating officer" comparison is useful because it separates two kinds of work that couples usually blur: running the household, and doing the household tasks.

What is a household COO?

The household COO is whoever is responsible for operations: knowing what needs to happen, making sure it happens, and noticing when it has not. In a company, the COO does not personally do every job. They make sure the jobs are assigned, on schedule and working.

At home, that looks like:

  • Keeping the calendar: school terms, appointments, birthdays, renewals.
  • Planning meals and keeping the food supply going.
  • Managing suppliers: the cleaner, the plumber, the childminder, the nursery.
  • Tracking bills, deadlines and paperwork.
  • Noticing what is running out, wearing out or overdue.
  • Coordinating the people: who is where, who needs what, and when.

That list is a fair description of the mental load. Allison Daminger's research on household cognitive labour described the same work as anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding and monitoring. The COO framing simply gives it a job title. What the mental load is explains it without the business language.

Why does an unnamed COO cause problems?

Because a role that nobody agreed to is a role that nobody can hand over, limit or recognise.

In most households, one person drifted into the job. Perhaps they were at home more during a parental leave, or they were the first to notice the bins had not gone out, or they were simply more bothered by things being left undone. The role grew around them without a conversation.

Three problems follow from that.

  1. The work is not counted. The COO's time goes on planning, which leaves no trace. The other partner sees the visible tasks and assumes the split is roughly even.
  2. The other partner becomes staff. They do what they are asked, when they are asked. They are often willing and capable, but they are not in charge of anything, so the COO is never off duty.
  3. Burnout builds quietly. Running a household in your head on top of a job, with no time off, is a common route to exhaustion. The cost of being the household manager covers that risk.

Should one partner be the household COO?

Usually not for everything, and not permanently. A single COO for the whole household recreates the original problem with a label on it. Two better options work for most couples.

Option 1: split the COO role by area

Each partner is COO of a few areas, end to end. Priya and Tom both work full-time. Priya is COO of the children: school, clubs, health appointments, birthday parties. Tom is COO of the house and money admin: bills, insurance, repairs, the food shop. Each is fully responsible for their own areas, including the remembering, and the other only steps in when asked.

Option 2: rotate the whole role

One partner is COO for a set period, often three months, and then hands over. This works well for couples who want both people to understand the whole household. It needs a proper handover, written down, or the outgoing COO keeps doing the job in their head.

How do you set up a household COO role?

Write the job description, split it, and give the new arrangement a month before judging it.

  1. List the operations. Each of you separately writes down everything that has to be planned or tracked in a normal month. Combine the lists. Most couples are surprised by the length.
  2. Group them into areas. Children, food, house, money, social, health. Six or seven areas is typical.
  3. Assign an owner per area. One name each. "Both" means nobody.
  4. Agree the standard. What does "done" look like for each area? Agree the real standards; let the rest be the owner's choice.
  5. Agree what the other partner does. Tasks can still be shared out. The COO of food might ask the other partner to collect a click-and-collect order. That is fine, as long as the owner remains the one holding the plan.
  6. Review after a month. Is the load even? Is anyone still doing someone else's area in their head?

The difference between doing a task and owning an area is the whole point. Helping out versus taking ownership covers this in depth.

What goes wrong with the COO approach?

The COO becomes a manager of the other partner, rather than of the household. That is the most common way the approach goes wrong, and it is worth naming in advance.

A COO of the house who checks on how their partner loaded the dishwasher has turned operations into supervision. Supervision is not shared work. It is more work for the supervisor, and it feels like being managed for the other person. If you are COO of an area, you run that area. You do not run your partner.

The second pitfall is not counting the role as work. A COO who also does an equal share of the physical chores is doing more, not the same. When you check the balance, count planning time alongside doing time. Common questions

Isn't it unromantic to run a household like a business?

The language is a tool, not a lifestyle. Borrowing one idea from business, that every operation needs a named owner, usually reduces the number of tense conversations about logistics, which leaves more room for everything else.

What if my partner does not want to be COO of anything?

Start with one area they already care about, such as the car or the food shop, and give it to them completely. Owning one area well is a better start than reluctantly sharing everything.

Can the household COO role be outsourced?

Some tasks can be paid for, such as cleaning or meal delivery. The coordinating role usually cannot be fully outsourced, because someone still has to manage the help.

Should the partner who works fewer hours be the COO?

They may reasonably take more of it, but by explicit agreement, and with the planning time counted. The fair test is whether both partners end up with a similar amount of genuinely free time.
