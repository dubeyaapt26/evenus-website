Mothers carry most of the mental load mainly because of how families are set up, not because of anything innate. The parent who takes longer leave becomes the expert on the baby, schools and other parents contact mothers first, society judges mothers for a missed appointment or a messy house, and every year of practice widens the skill gap. None of these is a choice anyone made, which is why the pattern is so stubborn. It can change, but only if both parents change the defaults on purpose.

The pattern is common in different-sex couples, and research on couples consistently finds that women do more unpaid domestic work on average. It is not universal, and in many families a father, or one of two mums or two dads, is the default parent. What follows applies to whoever holds that role. If you are new to the term itself, what the mental load is explains it plainly.

Is the mental load on mothers biological?

No. The evidence points to arrangement, not biology. Noticing that the baby needs a bigger size of clothes, or that the nursery form is due on Friday, is a learned skill built through repetition. It is not something one parent is born with.

One useful clue comes from same-sex couples. They also have to work out who plans and who notices, and they often divide it more evenly because there is no gendered default to fall back on. That does not mean they never end up with one default parent. It means the role is assigned by circumstance rather than assumed. Why same-sex couples divide housework more evenly goes into what other couples can learn from that.

Why do mums end up with the mental load?

Five forces push it towards mothers, and each one reinforces the others. Whether you say mums or moms, the pattern is the same.

1. Parental leave creates an expert

In many families, the mother takes longer leave after a birth. During those months she learns the feeding patterns, the health visitor schedule, which sleep suit fits, and which cry means what. When both parents are back at work, the knowledge stays with her. Asking her becomes faster than finding out, so everyone asks her.

2. Institutions contact mothers first

Schools, nurseries, doctors' surgeries and other parents often message the mother by default. The class WhatsApp group fills up with mums. Each message is a small piece of mental load that arrives in one person's phone, and nobody else knows it exists.

3. Judgement falls unevenly

If a child turns up to school without their PE kit, people tend to look at the mother. If the house is messy when guests arrive, it is often seen as reflecting on her. That judgement makes letting things slip feel riskier for mothers, so they keep holding the details, even when they would like to let go.

4. Skill gaps compound

Each year of practice makes the default parent faster and the other parent less familiar. By the time children are at school, handing over the mental load means explaining years of context. The explanation feels like more work than just doing it, so the handover never happens.

5. "Helping" keeps her as the manager

Many fathers do a lot of hands-on parenting and housework. But if they do it when asked, the mother is still the one who notices, plans and asks. Arlie Hochschild called the unpaid work after a paid day "the second shift" in her 1989 book of that name. The mental load is the part of the second shift that does not end when the jobs are done. Why "just ask for help" is part of the problem explains that dynamic.

What does this look like in a real family?

It usually looks fair on the surface. Take Priya and Tom, both working full-time, with two children aged four and seven.

Tom does the school run four mornings a week, cooks at weekends and does bath time. On a chore chart, he looks like an equal partner, and in hours of physical work he nearly is.

Priya, meanwhile, is on the school email list, the class group chat and the GP's text reminders. She knows when the vaccinations are due, which child has a party on Saturday, what the present budget is, and when the school uniform needs replacing. She also notices that the older child has been quiet since half term.

Tom is doing a lot. Priya is running the system that tells Tom what to do. When Priya says she is exhausted, Tom points to his share of the chores, and both of them are telling the truth.

Why does it matter?

Because the mental load affects a mother's free time, career and wellbeing well beyond the minutes it takes. A parent who is always holding the schedule rarely has a truly free evening, even when nothing is happening. Over years this can feed into burnout. The cost of being the household manager looks at that risk. If exhaustion has started to affect your mood, sleep or health, please talk to your doctor or a counsellor.

It also matters for the other parent. A father who never holds the mental load misses out on knowing his children's lives in detail: their friends, their worries, the small things that come up at school.

How to change the narrative

Change the defaults too, not only the division of tasks. Here are the changes that make the biggest difference.

  1. Change the contact details. Put the father's phone number first with the school, nursery and GP. Add him to the class group. This one change moves a surprising amount.
  2. Hand over a whole area. School admin, health appointments or birthday parties, including the noticing. Not "help with" it. Own it.
  3. Use leave to build knowledge. If the father can take leave alone, even a couple of weeks, he becomes an expert in his own right. This works best early on, in the first year. Protecting balance in the first year covers how to plan for it.
  4. Let standards differ. If he packs a different lunch, that is fine. Correcting it pulls the area back.
  5. Drop the language of babysitting. A father looking after his own children is parenting. Small words shape who is seen as responsible.

Seeing it in numbers can help when the conversation stalls. EvenUS counts the planning and remembering as hours alongside the chores, so a chore list that looks even but is not shows up as uneven. It never says whose fault that is.

Common questions

Do working mothers carry more mental load than stay-at-home mothers?

Not necessarily more, but they have less time to absorb it. A parent at home may reasonably take more of the day-to-day, though the evening and weekend load should still be shared.

What if my partner says I am better at it?

You probably are, because you have had years of practice. That is a reason to hand some of it over, not to keep it.

Does the mental load get lighter as children grow up?

It changes shape. Nappies give way to exams, lifts, money and teenage worries, which can be just as demanding to keep track of.

Is this only a problem in different-sex couples?

No. Any couple can fall into one default parent. The gendered pattern simply makes it more likely to happen without anyone noticing.
