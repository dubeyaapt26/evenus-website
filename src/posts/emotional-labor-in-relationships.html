Emotional labour in a relationship is the effort of managing feelings so that things run smoothly: keeping the peace, reading moods, softening bad news, remembering what matters to people, and holding back your own reaction when the moment is wrong. It is usually carried by whoever is best at noticing feelings, and in many different-sex couples that falls to the woman by default rather than by agreement. The term began as a description of paid work, and it still helps to know where it came from.

What does emotional labour mean?

Emotional labour means managing emotions as part of getting something done. The sociologist Arlie Hochschild introduced the idea in her 1983 book The Managed Heart, which looked at flight attendants. Their job was not only to serve drinks. It was to stay warm and calm with difficult passengers, to look relaxed during turbulence, and to produce a feeling in other people as part of the service.

Hochschild was describing a workplace bargain: employers were paying for feelings, and the worker paid for it in effort and, sometimes, in a sense of distance from their own emotions. In her original meaning, emotional labour is something a job demands of you.

Since then the phrase has travelled. People now use it for the unpaid version at home: the work of keeping a relationship, a family or a friendship emotionally on track. You will see it spelled "emotional labor" in American writing. The meaning has stretched, and some people argue it now covers too much. That is a fair point, so it helps to be specific about what you mean when you use it.

What does emotional labour look like in a relationship?

It looks like a series of small judgements and adjustments that nobody sees being made. Most of it happens before anyone speaks.

  • Mood monitoring. Noticing your partner is quiet after a call with their manager, and changing your plans for the evening because of it.
  • Timing. Deciding that tonight is not the night to raise the credit card bill, and carrying the topic for another three days.
  • Translating. Explaining one person's reaction to another. "He's not angry with you, he's worried about his dad."
  • Kin-keeping. Remembering birthdays, anniversaries and who is not speaking to whom on both sides of the family, and sending the message that keeps everyone connected.
  • Self-editing. Rewording a complaint three times so it will not start an argument.
  • Absorbing. Staying calm when someone else is not, so the temperature in the room comes down.

None of this is bad in itself. Caring about someone's feelings is part of loving them. It becomes a problem when one person is doing nearly all of it, all the time, and the other has never had to notice.

Emotional labour vs mental load vs housework

These are three different kinds of work, and they are often carried by the same person. Separating them makes the conversation much easier.

Type of workWhat it isExample
Housework (physical chores)Doing the taskCooking dinner, cleaning the bathroom
Mental load (cognitive labour)Planning, remembering, deciding, checkingKnowing there is nothing in for dinner and that Tuesday is the only free evening
Emotional labourManaging feelings, yours and other people'sSensing your partner has had a bad day and deciding not to mention the dinner plans yet

Remembering your mother-in-law's birthday is mental load. Choosing the card, phoning her, and smoothing over the fact that you forgot last year is emotional labour. The full comparison is in emotional labour versus mental load, and what the mental load is covers the logistics side on its own.

Who carries the emotional labour in most relationships?

Usually the person who learned to notice first, and that pattern often follows gender. Many women are raised to read the room and keep the peace, so in different-sex couples the work tends to drift to them without anyone deciding it. The same drift happens in same-sex couples and friendships. It goes to whoever is more attuned, and being attuned then quietly becomes their job.

Three things keep it in place:

  1. Skill compounds. The person who reads moods well gets more practice, gets better, and becomes the obvious person to do it next time.
  2. It is invisible when it works. A calm evening looks like nothing happened. Nobody sees the three arguments that were defused before they started.
  3. It is hard to hand over. You can give someone the laundry. It is much harder to give them the job of noticing that your sister sounds low.

Research on couples consistently finds that women in heterosexual relationships do more unpaid domestic work on average, the "second shift" Hochschild described in a later book. Emotional work is harder to measure than housework, so be careful with anyone who gives you a precise figure for it. The pattern is real. A number is usually invented.

How do you know if you carry most of the emotional labour?

Look at what happens when you stop. If relationships with family go quiet, tension builds without anyone addressing it, and birthdays pass unmarked, you were holding them up.

A quick check. Answer yes or no for each:

  • You are the one who notices when your partner is stressed, and they rarely notice when you are.
  • You plan how to say difficult things. Your partner tends to just say them.
  • You keep in touch with both sides of the family.
  • You smooth things over after arguments, even ones you did not start.
  • You feel tired after social events that your partner found relaxing.
  • You have stopped raising some things because the reaction costs more than the issue.

Four or more yeses suggests the work is lopsided. The last one deserves the most attention, because it is how imbalance turns into distance. Hidden resentment from unequal invisible labour goes into how that happens.

How to share emotional labour more fairly

Make it specific, then move the parts that can actually move. Emotional labour cannot be split on a rota, but a surprising amount of it is attached to concrete jobs that can change hands.

Name instances, not a category

"You never do the emotional labour" is a statement about character, and it starts a defence. "I have sent every birthday message to your side of the family for six years" is a fact, and facts can change by next month. Take Sam and Alex. Sam realised that every message in Alex's family group chat over the past year had been started by Sam. They agreed Alex's family was Alex's to keep in touch with, starting with his nephew's birthday that Friday.

Give each person their own side

Kin-keeping is the easiest piece to divide: your family, your cards, your calls, your awkward conversations. It feels strange for a few months and then becomes normal.

Say what you need out loud

Part of the imbalance is that one partner reads unspoken needs and the other waits to be told. The fix runs both ways. The attuned partner says "I need you to ask how I am tonight" without feeling it should not have to be said. The other partner practises asking without being prompted.

Agree on how you raise hard things

If one person is always managing the timing of difficult conversations, set a regular slot so nobody has to judge the mood. A fixed weekly conversation removes a whole layer of emotional management. Emotional labour in marriage looks at how this plays out between spouses over the years.

Can you measure emotional labour?

Not honestly, and it is better not to try. You can estimate how long a chore takes. You cannot put a number on comforting someone at 11pm without turning care into a transaction, and once it is a transaction it stops feeling like care.

This is why EvenUS deliberately does not score emotional labour. It measures time, money and the mental load of household tasks, where an estimate is meaningful, and leaves feelings out of the numbers. What you can do is reduce the practical work that sits underneath a lot of emotional strain, so there is more room for the conversations that do need a person.

Common questions

Is emotional labour the same as emotional support?

Not quite. Emotional support is comfort you give someone because they need it. Emotional labour is the wider effort of managing feelings to keep things running, including your own, and it becomes a strain when it only flows one way.

Is it unfair to expect a partner to manage your feelings?

Some of it is a normal part of any close relationship. It becomes unfair when one person is consistently managing both people's emotions and their own needs never get the same attention.

Can men carry most of the emotional labour?

Yes. The work goes to whoever is more attuned, and in some couples that is the man. The gendered pattern is common, not universal.

Should we see a counsellor about it?

If conversations about it keep turning into the same argument, a couples counsellor can help both of you hear each other. That is a good option, not a last resort.
