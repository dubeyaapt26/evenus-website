Emotional labour in a relationship is the effort of managing feelings so that things run smoothly: keeping the peace, reading moods, softening bad news, remembering what matters to people, and holding back your own reaction when the moment is wrong. It is usually carried by whoever is best at noticing feelings, and in many different-sex couples that falls to the woman by default rather than by agreement. In a long marriage it tends to build up quietly over the years, which is why it is worth naming early.

What does emotional labour mean?

Emotional labour means managing emotions as part of getting something done. The sociologist Arlie Hochschild introduced the idea in her 1983 book The Managed Heart, which looked at flight attendants. Serving drinks was the visible part. Staying warm with a rude passenger and looking relaxed during turbulence was the rest of the job, and nobody wrote it on the rota.

Since then the phrase has travelled. People now use it for the unpaid version at home: the work of keeping a relationship, a family or a friendship emotionally on track. Some people think the word has been stretched too far, so it's worth being specific about what you mean by it.

What it looks like in a relationship

Mostly it's small judgements and adjustments that nobody sees you make. In a marriage they tend to fall into five areas.

Moods

  • Noticing your partner is quiet after a call with their manager, and changing your plans for the evening because of it.
  • Absorbing stress about their job, night after night, without the same space for yours.
  • Staying calm when someone else is not, so the temperature in the room comes down.

Family

  • Remembering birthdays, anniversaries and who is not speaking to whom on both sides of the family, and sending the message that keeps everyone connected. This is often called kin-keeping.
  • Deciding where you spend Diwali or Christmas, then handling whichever side is disappointed.
  • Translating one person's reaction to another: "He's not angry with you, he's worried about his dad."

Conflict

  • Deciding that tonight is not the night to raise the credit card bill, and carrying the topic for another three days.
  • Rewording a complaint three times so it will not start an argument.
  • Being the one who reopens the conversation after a row, every time.

Children

  • Being the parent the children bring their worries to, and then deciding what to pass on.
  • Explaining one parent's bad mood to the children so they do not take it personally.

The shared future

  • Noticing that you have not had a proper evening together in two months.
  • Raising the hard topics: a second child, a move, an ageing parent, money worries.

Most of this is simply what caring about someone looks like. The trouble starts when it flows mostly one way, and one of you has never had to notice.

Emotional labour vs mental load vs housework

These are three different kinds of work, and they are often carried by the same person.

Type of workWhat it isExample
Housework (physical chores)Doing the taskCooking dinner, cleaning the bathroom
Mental load (cognitive labour)Planning, remembering, deciding, checkingKnowing there is nothing in for dinner and that Tuesday is the only free evening
Emotional labourManaging feelings, yours and other people'sSensing your partner has had a bad day and deciding not to mention the dinner plans yet

Remembering your mother-in-law's birthday is mental load. Choosing the card, phoning her and smoothing over the fact that you forgot last year, is emotional labour. A lot of emotional management is really about avoiding a practical argument, such as when to mention that the car insurance has not been renewed, so fixing the practical side often removes some of the emotional side. The full comparison is in emotional labour versus mental load, and what the mental load is covers the logistics side on its own.

Who carries the emotional labour in most relationships?

Usually the person who learned to notice first, and that pattern often follows gender. Many women are raised to read the room, so in different-sex couples the work drifts to them without anyone deciding it. In same-sex couples, and some where it is the man, it goes to whoever is more attuned, and being attuned quietly becomes their job.

Once it lands with one person, it tends to stay there, for a few reasons.

  1. Skill compounds. Whoever reads moods well gets more practice and becomes the obvious person to do it.
  2. It is invisible when it works. Nobody sees the arguments defused before they started.
  3. It is hard to hand over. You can hand over the laundry, but not easily the job of noticing that your sister sounds low.

Research consistently finds that women in heterosexual relationships do more unpaid domestic work on average, the "second shift" Hochschild described in a later book. Emotional work is harder to measure, so be wary of anyone quoting a precise figure for it.

How emotional labour builds up in a marriage

It rarely starts out unequal. It tips a little further at each big life change, and nobody resets it afterwards. Here's a composite example of how that tends to go.

Priya and Tom married in their late twenties. At first things felt balanced: each handled their own friends and family.

Year three: the first baby. Priya took the longer leave. She became the expert on the baby's moods, then on everyone's. Tom's parents started calling Priya for updates.

Year five: Tom's promotion. Priya started managing the evenings around his energy: when dinner was, when to raise anything difficult, how loud the children could be.

Year eight: Tom's father becomes unwell. Priya organised the visits, handled her mother-in-law's worries on the phone, and translated Tom's silence to his family.

Year nine. Priya realised she hadn't raised anything for herself in about a year, because there was never a good time. Each step had made sense when it happened, but nobody had chosen where they ended up. Big changes such as caring for ageing parents load more emotional work onto whoever is already doing it, because they are the one who already knows how.

Why it matters

The cost shows up in how close you feel to each other, more than in hours. Someone who manages their partner's feelings for years, without much coming back, can start to feel alone with that person sitting right next to them. A few things tend to follow.

The first is silence. The person carrying the load stops raising problems, because managing the reaction is more work than living with the problem.

Then resentment, which builds slowly and comes out over something small, like an unanswered text or a forgotten bin day. Hidden resentment from unequal invisible labour goes into how that happens.

And intimacy suffers. It's hard to feel close to someone you're managing. How unequal chores and mental load affect intimacy covers this in more depth.

The partner being managed often has no idea it's happening. So when the subject finally comes up, years of grievance arrive at once and it feels like an ambush. Usually neither of them set it up this way; the arrangement grew while nobody was looking at it.

Checking whether most of it sits with you

Notice what happens when you stop. If the family group chat goes quiet and tension builds with nobody addressing it, you were the one holding it up. Answer yes or no to each of these:

  • You are the one who notices when your partner is stressed, and they rarely notice when you are.
  • You plan how to say difficult things. Your partner tends to just say them.
  • You keep in touch with both sides of the family.
  • You smooth things over after arguments, even ones you did not start.
  • You have stopped raising some things because the reaction costs more than the issue.

Three or more yeses suggests the work is lopsided. Pay most attention to the last one, since that's usually where imbalance starts turning into distance.

How to share emotional labour more fairly

Make it specific, then move the parts that can actually move. You can't put emotional labour on a rota, but a surprising amount of it is tied to concrete jobs that can change hands.

Name instances, not a category

"You never do the emotional labour" sounds like a comment on someone's character, and they'll defend themselves. "I've sent every birthday message to your side of the family for six years" is a fact, and it's one you can change by next month. Sam realised every message in Alex's family group chat that year had been started by Sam, so they agreed Alex's family was Alex's to keep in touch with, starting with his nephew's birthday that Friday.

Each of you owns your own family

Birthdays, calls, gifts, hospital visits and difficult conversations with your own parents and siblings. This is the single biggest transfer most couples can make.

Agree a fixed time for hard conversations

If one of you is always picking the moment for difficult conversations, set a regular slot instead. Twenty minutes on Sunday evening, every week, whatever the mood, and then nobody has to judge the timing.

Take turns reopening after a row

If one of you always goes first, agree that the other goes first next time.

Say what you need, and ask directly

Part of the imbalance is that one partner reads unspoken needs and the other waits to be told, so both of you have something to change. The attuned partner says "I need you to ask how I am tonight" without feeling awkward that it needed saying. The other partner practises asking "How are you, actually?" without being prompted.

Hand over one part of a child's emotional world

For example, one parent takes on the friendship worries at school for a term, including the conversations with other parents.

Don't try to sort it all out in one evening. Pick one transfer, such as each person handling their own family, and give it two months before adding another. For the conversation that starts all this, dividing labour without blame or guilt has a structure that keeps it from becoming an argument about who is the better partner.

Can you measure emotional labour?

Not honestly, and it's better not to try. You can estimate how long a chore takes. Scoring the time you spent comforting someone at 11pm turns care into a ledger. The point is that you both notice and you both feel looked after, and a tally that matches won't give you that.

This is why EvenUS deliberately does not score emotional labour. It measures time, money and the mental load of household tasks, where an estimate is meaningful, and leaves feelings out of the numbers. Taking some of the practical work off the table, the stuff that sits underneath a lot of emotional strain, leaves more room for the conversations that need the two of you.

Common questions

Is emotional labour the same as emotional support?

Not quite. Emotional support is comfort given when someone needs it. Emotional labour is the wider effort of managing feelings, including your own, to keep things running.

Is it normal for one partner to be more emotionally attuned?

Yes, and different strengths are fine. It only becomes an issue when being attuned turns into a job that never gets a day off, and one person's needs never get the same attention.

What if my partner says I am overthinking it?

Keep to specific facts rather than the concept, such as who sent the last ten messages to their family.

Is emotional labour a reason couples divorce?

Many people describe feeling alone in a marriage long before it ends, and one-sided emotional work is often part of that. It is rarely the only reason, and it is something couples can change if they name it early.

Does emotional labour change after children leave home?

It often shifts rather than shrinks, towards ageing parents and adult children. It is a good moment to divide things again.

Should we see a counsellor about it?

If conversations about it keep turning into the same argument, a couples counsellor can help both of you hear each other.
